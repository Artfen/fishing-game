# Slow Tide

**Play it: <https://fishing-game-weld.vercel.app>**

A small, peaceful fishing adventure that runs in the browser. You start in a little wooden boat on Willow Bay, step ashore at a landing, walk up to your camp, and fish a freshwater lake and a clear trout stream. Beyond the bay lies a whole coast: the Wend estuary with its sandbanks and saltings, Longmere (a big inland lake with a boathouse), Gannet Isle with its lighthouse and kelp reefs, a long offshore shoal, and the deep Blue Water where tuna chase the sprat shoals and blue sharks, makos and albacore roam. Nine boats get you about: the rowing boat, a motor skiff, a quiet kayak, a bass boat, a little cabin boat, a sailing boat, a fast centre-console boat, a RIB (a rigid inflatable) and a sea-going sport-fishing yacht; and for the fun of it, a windsurf board and a jet ski. On the bay's north shore is **Willowhaven**, a little harbour with a quay, a marina pontoon (with a finger of your own and a visitors' row), a harbour office, a chandlery, a notice board, a fish market and an inn, The Lobster Pot; round the shore from it lie a rocky headland, a secluded beach and, under a buoy offshore, an old wreck. Up the lane above Shell Strand is **home**: a cottage and a garage with a workbench for planning an outing, your rods and camping gear, and a car on the drive to take you to the coast. You choose your rods and tackle, cook what you catch, pitch your own camp wherever the ground allows, sleep in a tent (or your own bed) until the hour you like, and keep a handwritten journal, a photo album and a map of it all.

The water is a living place: weed beds, rocks, lily pads, drowned trees, reefs, sandy flats, channels, deep water and running pools, home to some two thousand fish, cephalopods and eels of 62 kinds: 52 real species of the Atlantic coast from Royan to Cádiz to catch (the blue shark, the mako and the albacore of the Blue Water among them, and a thornback ray, a catshark, the venomous weever, hake and the John Dory), nine freshwater fish, and the European eel, protected, to watch. Across the open Atlantic to the south lies **Las Brasas**, a volcanic island in the trade wind with clear blue water and nineteen kinds of its own, marlin, sailfish and wahoo among them: sail (or motor) there yourself, the whole way, or take the fast passage. They school, cruise, feed, rest by cover, hunt, hold station in the current and scatter when startled. Fresh water, brackish water and the sea each have their own. Wind, swell and currents shape the water you fish, gently in the bays and more out on the open coast. Rings, flashes, jumps, fizzing bubbles, boiling water and diving seabirds on the surface come from real fish below. You can look underwater, follow your bait or lure, and use a fish finder. The fish that takes your bait is one you could have watched swimming there.

It's a static website: plain JavaScript modules, with no framework or bundler. Everything it needs ships in this folder, including [three.js](https://threejs.org) and the fonts, so it makes no requests to other sites. It installs as an app and plays offline (see [Offline play](#offline-play)). The only server code is one small optional function for cloud saves, which stay switched off until you set them up (see [Cloud saves](#cloud-saves-optional)); the same backend lets friends [play together](#playing-together-optional).

## Run it

```bash
python3 serve.py
```

Then open <http://localhost:8642>. `serve.py` is a tiny static server with caching turned off, so a browser refresh always picks up your edits. It also answers the game's cloud-save question with "not set up", so locally you always play as a guest. On `localhost` the game skips its offline cache so your edits always show; add `?sw` to the address (<http://localhost:8642/?sw>) to try offline play locally. Any static server works too.

## Put it online

**Vercel** (what the live game uses): the project is imported from GitHub with the framework preset *Other*. `vercel.json` tells Vercel to run `node tools/build-sw.mjs` on every deploy (it writes the list of game files, and a version made from their contents, into `sw.js` for offline play) and to serve the repository root. Every push to `main` redeploys the site, and every other branch gets its own preview address, so you can try a branch before merging it. No environment variables are needed, unless you want [cloud saves](#cloud-saves-optional). The `api/` folder holds the one optional function for those; Vercel picks it up by itself.

Any other static host works too. Run `node tools/build-sw.mjs` first (so offline play picks up your changes), then upload these files and folders, keeping the layout as it is:

```
index.html  style.css  manifest.webmanifest  sw.js  src/  vendor/  assets/
```

`serve.py`, `README.md`, `tools/`, `research/`, `supabase/` and `.claude/` are for working locally and don't need to go up. `api/` only works on Vercel; elsewhere the game simply stays guest-only. Some other easy hosts:

- **Netlify Drop** (app.netlify.com/drop) or **Cloudflare Pages**: drag the folder in and you get a web address within a minute.
- **GitHub Pages**: set Pages to deploy from the `main` branch, root folder.
- **itch.io**: make a zip, create a project with *Kind of project: HTML*, upload the zip and tick *This file will be played in the browser*.

```bash
node tools/build-sw.mjs && zip -r slow-tide.zip index.html style.css manifest.webmanifest sw.js src vendor assets
```

**Link previews:** the `og:url` and `og:image` lines in `index.html` point at the Vercel address. If you move the game to another site, change them to the new full address; most social sites ignore relative image paths.

**Privacy:** the game loads nothing from other sites, uses no cookies and no analytics. Progress is kept in the player's own browser (local storage) and never leaves their device. If you switch on cloud saves and a player chooses to sign in, their save (and email address) is also stored in your own Supabase project, where only they can read it. If a player starts or joins a session to [play together](#playing-together-optional), their chosen name, where they are and what they catch go through your Supabase project's Realtime service to the others in that session while it lasts; none of it is stored.

## How to play

With a keyboard and mouse (every key below can be changed in **Settings → Controls & comfort**; keys are shown as printed on your keyboard, so W A S D is Z Q S D on AZERTY):

| Key | Does |

**Expert technique** (Settings → Controls & comfort → *Technique*; `src/technique.js`). On *Assisted* (the default) a lure is worked with a few clear controls (hold to reel at a chosen speed, tap to twitch, let go to pause) and a fish is struck with a press. On *Expert* the rod itself is in your hands: on a controller the **right stick** holds the rod (up lifts it, down drops it, across sweeps it to the side) and the **right trigger** is the reel, winding as fast as you squeeze it; on a keyboard, forward and back lift and drop the rod and the reel key winds at an even pace.
- **Working a lure.** Lifting the rod draws the lure through the water as far as the tip travels (about a metre and a half for a full lift), and dropping it gives slack, so a sinking lure flutters down. A sharp snap is a twitch the fish see; a slow lift isn't. A **popper** spits and gulps on a short snap down. A **stick bait** steps to one side with each sideways flick and, flicked side to side in an even rhythm, walks the dog (the readout counts the rhythm; an even one draws fish up from further down). A **jig** rises as you lift and falls back on the slack.
- **The strike.** Sweep the rod up hard as a fish takes and it strikes: the harder the sweep, the firmer the hook goes home (a firm strike often turns a light hold solid; a feeble one can leave a solid hold light). A press still strikes, as on Assisted.
- **Playing a fish.** Pump it in: lift the rod while it rests to draw it towards you, then lower it as you wind (pumping lands a beaten fish noticeably sooner, and lifting into a running fish loads the line instead). A raised rod bends and cushions a surge; pointed at the fish it passes the jolt straight to the line. Held right up over a fish close in, the rod tip takes all the strain (high-sticking). The rod's sweep is your side pressure.
- **Readouts.** While a lure or jig is out, the hint line shows its depth and its speed through the water.

It's the same lure and the same fish as Assisted: the rod only moves the lure, sets the hook and loads the line, so better technique shows as better presentation and fewer fish lost, never as bites from nowhere. *Old salt* turns it on.

**How do you like to play?** Asked once when you begin (`src/playstyle.js`), and in Settings → Controls & comfort → *Play style*. One choice sets the fight's difficulty, how the fish behave, casting, starting the engine, the throttle and the economy together:

**Feel it through the controller.** Slow Tide is meant to be played on a controller, and a lot of it comes through your hands (`src/haptics.js`). Aboard, it isn't a hum all the while: you feel the engine fire as it starts, the lever click into each notch (a firmer detent at neutral) and clunk into gear, a swell as she digs in when you open the throttle (heavier on the big inboards, next to nothing from an electric motor), a sea slamming under the bow at speed, and a knock against a pier, a boat or the bottom; in the car, a swell as you put your foot down; and the thunder. Fishing: the lure whipping off on the cast and landing; a nibble's tick, a bite's tap and a predator's strike; the hook going home; the flick before a run and the run itself; the fish's weight on the line, all through the fight, heavier as the line nears its limit; the drag buzzing as it gives line, faster the harder the fish takes it; a turn, a jump, a head-shake, the creak at the line's limit, the snap, a fish coming off, the last kick alongside, and the fish in the net. On an Xbox controller in Chrome or Edge the reel trigger carries the fish too: its weight and the drag's buzz under your finger. Steady feelings fade out the moment they stop, a menu or the pause stops everything at once, and nothing in the game depends on it.

**Hear where things are.** Around a harbour, sounds come from where they really are (`src/earshot.js`). The sea slaps against the quay wall and clops under the pontoon nearest you. Mooring lines creak and fenders squeak on the boats lying there, busier in a swell, and a halyard taps a mast in a breeze. Gulls call from over the harbour and from where birds are working. A boat going out or coming in is heard from her side, her engine rising and fading as she passes. The talk in the inn spills out of its door. Each sound is panned to its side, turns as you turn, grows quieter and duller with distance, and is muffled through the walls when you're indoors. Headphones bring it out best.

Nothing can go wrong. A missed bite just means another will come; a snapped line only loses that fish (and the game tells you why it broke); and you can never starve.

### Your first trip

A new game begins at home, in front of the cottage with the car on the drive, and offers a short guide to a first outing (you can say no, end it whenever you like, and start it again from the journal, under **Memories**). A small card in the corner shows one step at a time, in the words of whatever controls you're using, and a marker points the way:

1. **Plan at the workbench** in the garage: what you're going for, where, and the rod and tackle to pack.
2. **Drive to the water**: get in at the driver's door and follow the lane, or let the drive go by.
3. **Find a spot**: each place has one marked (the end of Willowhaven's quay, the surf at Shell Strand, the ledges at Cove Head…), with a word on where the fish are.
4. **Cast**, 5. **land a fish**, 6. **keep it or let it go** (it's in your journal either way),
7. and **home again**, or pitch a camp.

Each step is done when it actually happens, never on a timer, and doing a later one first is fine: skip the workbench and just drive off, and the guide catches up.

### A day in Slow Tide

1. Row across Willow Bay to **The Landing** on the west shore and press **E** to step ashore.
2. Walk up the path to **Camp**. Press **E** there to open it: swap rods and tackle with the tackle box, light the fire, grill and eat what you've kept, and sleep.
3. Walk on to **Reedmere**, the lake behind camp, or follow the path up **Fernbeck**, the stream beyond it.
4. Pick the right rod and tackle for the water (press **B**), fish, and keep a fish or two for supper.
5. Back at camp, grill your catch, eat, and sleep until dawn, morning, midday, afternoon, golden hour or night. While you sleep the light changes, the weather may turn, the fire burns down and the fish move to where the new hour suits them.
6. A marker always shows where your boat is. Walk up to it and press **E** to row home.

### Further afield

The coast is big: the far places are one to three minutes away by motor skiff, and you find them by going there. The **map** (**M**) fills in as you explore and marks every place you've found.

- **The Wend**: an estuary east of the bay, past the cove. Sandbanks and mudflats, reeds, a deep channel and the quiet channels of **The Saltings**. **Wend Quay** has a camp with a tackle box. The water is brackish: sea fish that like it (mullet, flounder, sea bass) and none of the lake fish.
- **Longmere**: a big lake inland, reached by the trail from Wend Quay. Weedy bays, a steep north shore where oaks have toppled into the water, an alder island (**Heron Holm**), and the **Mere Boathouse** with its jetty, the bass boat and a tackle box. Pike, perch, roach, and largemouth bass.
- **Gannet Isle**: a green island far out to the south-west, with the **Gannet Light** turning all night, kelp and reef all round it, and **Puffin Cove**, a sheltered landing with a camp and a tackle box. **The Skerries** lie halfway out. Wrasse, plaice, mackerel and, after dark, squid.
- **The Calf**: a small island just off **Heron Point**, about 600 m west of Willowhaven by boat (plan it at the workbench: the car takes you to the harbour, and you have a boat brought round to the quay). Tie up at the stone landing in its lee, climb the steps to a ruined **watch tower** that looks out over the whole coast, and camp on the top if the weather's kind (or sleep aboard the cabin boat in the lee). Grey seals haul out on the skerry. Between the Calf and the Point runs **Calf Sound**, the strongest tide race on the coast: white ridges, a current that carries a drifting boat east, and mackerel, garfish and bluefish hunting sandeels in it. Pollack and wrasse live over the kelp under the cliffs, and bass hunt the white water off **the Nose**, the low rocks at its south-east corner. A metal or a soft sandeel on the coastal rod, cast across the race.
- **Up the Coldwater to Hoarfell**: inland to the north-west the land rises to **Hoarfell**, 570 m, with snow on its top. Its river, the **Coldwater**, starts at **Hoar Tarn**, a cold, deep lake in a crag-backed corrie under the summit, runs down past the bothy and over **Spout Force** (thirty metres into a deep plunge pool), down the long wooded **Coldwater Dale**, over **the Lower Force** into the tide, and out into the bay under a timber footbridge at **Coldwater Foot**, just west of Willowhaven. A fingerpost by the footbridge points up the river: the path follows it all the way to the cairn on the top, 2.8 km on foot (about a quarter of an hour at a walk, and an hour or two of the game's day), round the side of the corrie and up the fell's north flank in long zigzags. **The bothy** above Spout Pool is a stone hut kept open for walkers: cook, sleep (you wake to the sound of the falls) and swap tackle at the door, and once you've walked there you can head straight back to it from the map. At the top, press **E** at the cairn to add a stone and look out over the whole coast; up there the haze lies below you. Trout and grayling live in the river's pools all the way up, small wild trout in the tarn, and sea bass, eels, flounder and mullet come up the tidal stretch as far as the Force Pool (deep enough for the kayak or a boat to follow them under the footbridge). The fell is built in detail only as you come near it, so the bigger land costs little until you're there.
- **Long Shoal**, marked by a yellow buoy, and **Little Skellig**, a rock covered in birds, on the way out to **The Blue Water**: open sea past the shelf, where tuna hunt the sprat shoals.

### Las Brasas: the crossing south

Across the open Atlantic to the south lies **Las Brasas**, a volcanic island in the trade wind, like the Canaries (an invented island, not a real one). It's in the same world as home, and the sea between is real: you can make **the crossing** at the helm the whole way, or take **the fast passage**.

- **Who can go.** The **sailing boat** and the **sport-fishing yacht**. Out on open water in either (past the bay), or standing beside her at her berth, press **E** and choose *the crossing south*. A card says what it takes before you set out: about twenty-five minutes at the helm in the sailing boat (twenty in the yacht) across four and a half kilometres of open sea from Gannet Light to the island, a day and more going by on the clock, through a night; the wind you'll have; the yacht's fuel (she needs 90 L aboard, and burns about 55 L across with the lever down); water and stores (taken aboard as you set out, and you eat and drink from them on the way); a gale due before you'd be across; and which way to steer. Anything smaller (the skiff, the console boat, the jet ski) is turned back at the edge of the home chart, with a word why. Sail a fit boat out past the edge without setting out and the card is offered; until you choose, she goes no further.
- **At the helm.** A marker shows where to steer for (Las Brasas, then **Punta del Faro**, then **Puerto Brasa**, the way round into the harbour on the island's south side), with the distance; the map (**M**) has a chart of its own for the open sea, both coasts on its edges and the route across, with you on it. The wind comes round as you go, from home's own weather through the north into the island's **north-east trade**, fresh and on the quarter going south; the coast's afternoon sea breeze fades away out there. Mid-ocean the **long Atlantic swell** runs bigger, and a motor boat can't be driven flat out in it: the yacht slams above about six knots, so she's held there. The sailing boat's speed is the wind's: about twenty-five minutes in a fresh trade wind, more in light airs or beating home into it.
- **On the way**, different every day you set out and the same for the same day: a big pod of **common dolphins** racing in to ride the bow, a **fin whale** blowing in the cooler northern half or a **sperm whale** in the warm southern half, **birds working** over tuna driving bait to the top (gannets, then Cory's shearwaters; troll a lure past the edge of it: the fish there are real, on the finder and on the hook), **flying fish** gliding away from the bow in the warm blue water, a **loggerhead turtle** up for a breath, a **squall** of rain and gusts, rafts of floating **sargassum weed** with dorado under them, a **ship** crossing ahead of you (a little container ship, deep-laden, on her way between the Channel and the islands: she won't turn for you, so keep well clear; push close and she gives you one long blast on her horn; after dark you see her lights, white over white and a red or a green); and quiet water between. Pico Brasa comes up over the horizon dead ahead, a cloud caught on its top, about two thirds of the way over.
- **If the weather turns** (a gale due, or blowing up), you choose: **reef** the sail (or ease the yacht back) and carry on, slower and easier; **turn back** while you're less than halfway; or **heave to** and let it pass, the hours going by.
- **Save and come back** whenever you like: the crossing, where you are and what's happened on it are kept, and you carry on from there. On a crossing, out on open water, **E** offers *the rest of the way by the fast passage*.
- **Landfall.** The day book writes it up (*Set out across the open Atlantic for Las Brasas in the sailing boat. … Made landfall at Las Brasas after the crossing: 27 minutes at the helm.*), and your record keeps your quickest crossing in each boat. Coming back is the same the other way: *the crossing home*, Gannet Light ahead.
- **The fast passage** is still there, plainly marked on the same card: a few lines of the log go by and you come in off Puerto Brasa (or to her berth at Willowhaven). The same hours go by as the crossing at the helm would take, the yacht burns the same fuel, and the day book has it on the day you arrive. Your gear and your creel come with you; the other boats and the car stay where they are (a boat you leave at Las Brasas stays there, until you sail or motor her home).

- **Puerto Brasa**: a little harbour behind a breakwater of big pale blocks with a green light flashing on its end, a dark basalt quay to tie up at (**Z** alongside), a **fuel pump** on the quay, a floating **pontoon** with finger berths down a gangway from it, white flat-roofed houses with coloured doors and shutters climbing the slope behind, a church with a bell gable, palms, little wooden boats pulled up and moored. **Bar El Muelle** on the waterfront has a room upstairs: cook what you've kept (the kitchen does the island's fish its own way), sleep, and swap tackle from the shelf. Once you've found it you can head straight back there from the map.
- **The island**: black lava and red cinder, **Pico Brasa** (330 m, a crater at the top) in the middle and **Montaña Roja**, a younger red cone, to the east; sea cliffs on the windward north coast; grey-green tabaiba scrub, candelabras of cardón, and Canary pines high on the volcano. **Playa Negra**, a beach of black sand, lies east of the harbour; **Punta del Faro**, the long east point, has a lighthouse; **Roque del Arco**, off the south-west, has a sea arch the swell runs through.
- **El Bajón**: the island's house reef, a short row west of the harbour mouth under a **yellow buoy**. Lava pinnacles stand up from about twenty metres to three, cragged with boulders and covered, as real Canary reefs are, in **sea fans** (yellow, orange, red and white gorgonians standing across the current), **sponges**, **orange sun coral**, anemones and brown algae. Clouds of **Azores chromis** hang over the tops, parrotfish and ornate wrasse graze them, **blacktail combers** watch from every ledge, triggerfish and trumpetfish along the sides, morays in the cracks, a dusky grouper in the cave at the foot, and a school of barracuda hangs over it all; white and two-banded bream, salema, octopus and squid too. The water is clear enough to see it from a boat floating over the pinnacles; dive it to see it properly.
- **The harbour** is full of fish you can see from the quay and the breakwater in the clear water: grey mullet along the quay, wrasse, two-banded bream, salema, combers and a cloud of chromis off the breakwater's blocks, an octopus and a moray in the mole, sand smelt over the sand in the middle, bogue and squid at the mouth, horse mackerel under the lights at night.
- **Five dive sites** at the depths they're known for, in your dive log with home's: **El Bajón** (3–20 m), **Los Arcos** off Roque del Arco (4–16 m: arches of old lava to swim through, groupers, morays and combers in the caves round their feet, barracuda and amberjack passing), **El Veril** south-east of the harbour (20–40 m and the blue below: the wall, groupers in its caves, red porgy and dentex on its ledges, amberjack along it, a sardine shoal for them to hunt), **El Sebadal** (8–14 m: the seagrass meadow, cuttlefish, sand smelt, salema grazing, striped bream and the angel sharks on the sand; its buoy is a mooring, so no anchor goes down in the seagrass) and **Las Coladas** on the windward north-west (4–14 m: lava reef, parrotfish, triggerfish, morays; dive it on a calm day). Each but El Bajón has a yellow buoy of its own (El Bajón's is the island's), and *Under the island* on the board at The Lobster Pot is for diving all five.
- **The hunters**: barracuda and amberjack drive the bogue, the chromis and the sardine to the top, and the bluefish, little tunny and bullet tuna hunt the island's own shoals: the sea boils, **Cory's shearwaters** come sweeping in low and drop onto the water among the fish, and the gulls with them. In the evening the shearwaters raft up on the sea off the Roque before they fly in to their burrows after dark. **Loggerhead turtles** drift in the lee, up for a breath now and then, curious about a still diver and off when a boat comes fast. The dolphins here notice a boat from further off.
- **Under the water**: clear, blue and warm, seeing twice as far as at home, and from a boat you can see the seabed and the fish down to a dozen metres or so. Black lava boulders grazed bare by long-spined urchins, brown weed in the shallows, the seagrass meadow of **El Sebadal** off the south-east, the rough reef of **Las Coladas** (old lava tongues on the north-west), more arches under the water off the Roque, sea fans, sponges and sun coral on the rock reefs below the surge, black coral down the walls, and **El Veril**, the drop-off, where the narrow shelf round the island falls away from twenty-odd metres into the deep blue.
- **The weather**: the trade wind blows from the north-east almost all the time, steady and seldom calm; the swell works on the windward north and west while the lee (the south, where the harbour is) stays calm; the Canary current runs south-west, a little faster off the points. Mostly fine: cloud now and then, a passing shower, very seldom a storm. After dark, shearwaters wail over the cliffs as they come in to their burrows.
- **The fish**: the home coast's fish that live there too (white and two-banded seabream, salema, bogue, red porgy and dentex, striped seabream and palometa off the beach, octopus in the lava, cuttlefish on the seagrass, grey mullet in the harbour, shoals of horse mackerel, sardine and bogue, bluefish, little tunny and bullet tuna off the drop-off; the field guide says where to look for each there), and twelve of the island's own: parrotfish, ornate wrasse, blacktail combers, triggerfish and morays on the lava reef, dusky groupers (to release), barracuda and amberjack along the drop-off, chub mackerel shoals, and the Azores chromis, the angelshark and the trumpetfish to watch (see [What's out there](#whats-out-there)). Ask Paco what's biting: he reads it off the island's fish as they are that hour.

The island has its own chart: open the map (**M**) there and it shows Las Brasas, filling in as you explore, as the home chart does at home. (Your camps, home, the car and the far camps are across the open sea: only the crossing takes you there.)

### Life on the coast

A path runs from the landing to **Willowhaven** and on round the shore. Each place has its own landmark, sound and fish, and something to find.

- **Willowhaven**: a stone quay with a light at its head, a slipway, cottages, the chandlery, The Lobster Pot, the fish market and a net store round a cobbled apron (see [The harbour](#the-harbour-the-fish-market-and-the-inn)). The **harbour office**, a little teal hut by the head of the quay, sees to your boats (press **E** at its hatch): which are in the water and which are laid up, one put back in the water or laid up, or one brought round and made fast alongside the quay (it takes a little while) to step down into from the quay steps. The **chandlery** door opens the whole tackle store, like a tackle box. The **notice board** reads the water as it is right now: the wind and sea inside and outside the bay, the current in the cove channel, where gulls are diving, which sea fish are busy at this hour round the harbour (from the fish actually living there) with a setup that suits each, and the notices that start adventures. The **bench** by the lamp lets you sit and let the time go by. Rigging clinks against the masts, busier in a breeze.
- **Cove Channel**: the sheltered way into Driftwood Cove, with eelgrass and a heron in the reeds. An old punt lies at the head of the cove.
- **Cove Head**: a rocky headland on the open coast with a white daymark tower. The swell breaks on its ledges, where bass and white bream feed.
- **Shell Strand**: a secluded beach south of the cove with a driftwood shelter and a dry sandy shelf above the waves, the best camping on the coast. Bass come into its surf at dusk; plaice and sole after dark.
- **The Kittiwake**: a wooden steam drifter lying on her side about nine metres down off Shell Strand, under a black-and-red isolated-danger buoy. Conger live in her hull and pollack and wrasse hang over her ribs. From a boat, the underwater view (**C**) lets you see her for yourself. (Her shape is kept so diving could visit her one day; there's no diving yet.)

### Your own camp

Your backpack carries a camping kit: a tent and bedroll, a lantern on a pole, a camp chair, a little stove, a tackle box and a cool box. Open the backpack (**B**), choose **Pitch a camp…**, and an outline of the camp follows the ground in front of you. It turns green on a good spot and red on a bad one, with the reason (in the water or where the waves reach, too steep to sleep on, a tree, rock or building in the way, across a path, in front of a doorway, on a jetty, too close to a camp, beyond the edge of the world), and warns you about low, exposed ground. The whole footprint is checked, not just the middle, and gentle slopes are fine: if the spot is nearly right the camp settles a step away where it fits. Confirm, give it a name (or keep the one offered), and it's pitched. Cancelling leaves the kit as it was.

At your camp, press **E** by the door:

- **Stove & food**: cook a fish from your creel or the cool box (half an hour), eat what's cooked, or have an oatcake.
- **Sleep**: until dawn, morning, midday, afternoon, golden hour or night. You wake at the tent door; your boats stay exactly where you left them and your rigs and catches are untouched. A fish on the line has to be landed or let go first, and a line left out is reeled in before you turn in.
- **Your camp**: rename it, choose the tent colour, set the lantern (after dark, always, or off), put out bunting, a rag rug and wind chimes (and the striped deckchair, once you've earned it), mark it on the map, open the journal or plan a trip from here, **Move this camp…** or **Pack it up…**.
- **Tackle box**: swap rods and tackle, as at any tackle box.

The cool box keeps six fish on ice and the stove holds four cooked ones. Moving a camp keeps its name, contents and history (it takes a little time). Packing up puts the tent back on your pack; if the cool box holds more than your creel can take, you choose which fish come with you and which go back into the water, and nothing goes without you saying so. You have one camping kit, so one camp of your own at a time, alongside the fixed camps. The backpack's **Head back to** list and the map's camp list both take you straight to it, and when you come back it's just as you left it.

Saved camps are checked when you load: if one somehow ends up somewhere it couldn't be pitched (after an update changed the ground, say), it's moved to the nearest good spot and the game tells you where and why.

The camping gear is three pieces, each in one place at a time: the **tent & bedroll** (with the lantern, chair and the camp's bits), the **stove & pans** and the **cool box**. You need the tent to pitch (if it's in the car close by, you fetch it); the stove and cool box come out of your backpack when you pitch, and a camp without them can't cook or keep fish on ice (its sheet says where they are, and sets them up when you bring them). Packing up, everything goes into your backpack.

### Home, the garage and the car

Home is a cottage and its garage on the east arm, up the lane above Shell Strand. Players who were already playing are offered it once (**Go home now** or **Later**) and otherwise carry on wherever they were; new games begin there. The map and the backpack's **Head back to** list take you there whenever you like.

- **The cottage**: your bed (sleep until the hour you choose), the desk with your journal and records, a kitchen (cook a fish from your creel and sit down to it), and a wall for three photographs, a keepsake and the record of a catch. The house fills it from what you've actually done (your latest photographs, your best catch, your newest keepsake), or you choose. Nothing is ever made up: empty frames stay empty.
- **The garage**: the tackle wall (a tackle box for your whole collection), the rod rack and the camping shelf (they show what's really there: a rod you carry or have loaded in the car leaves a gap), boots and bags, and the **workbench**.
- **The car**: a sage-green estate on the drive. Walk up to its door to drive, to its back to open the boot.

While you're indoors the roof lifts away (and the wall between you and the camera), so you can see what you're doing; in first person it stays put.

**The workbench** plans an outing, step by step but in any order: **what for** (fishing, for a fish you've met or a way of fishing; camping overnight; exploring and photographs), **where** (Willowhaven, Shell Strand and Cove Head by the lanes, Wend Quay and the Mere Boathouse by the road round the bay), **about it** (a map, the ground and the way in, the water and the fish that actually live there and how busy they are at this hour, your boats, the camping, the weather right now, which is never presented as a forecast), **gear** (setups that suit, from the field guide's own recommendations, packed into your backpack or loaded as spare tackle in the car; the camping gear between the garage, the car and your backpack), the **packing list** with a short check, and **off you go**. Places you haven't been yet keep their fish to themselves. Nothing has to be planned: "same again" takes last time's place and packing, loadouts of your own (six) are saved by name, and you can always just get in the car.

**Where things are**: everything you own is yours already (nothing is bought). What you carry is your backpack (two rods and eight baits or lures, as ever). The car's boot holds four more rods and twelve more tackle, and the camping gear. Everything else is on the tackle wall at home, and at the tackle boxes round the coast as before. Moving something never copies it: a rod in the car isn't on the wall, and packing a setup takes its pieces out of the car. At the boot you can swap tackle with your backpack, move the camping gear, and at home unload it all into the garage. Saved loadouts only name things: using one moves what it can and says what it can't (a tent still pitched at a camp, a car that isn't here).

**The check before you leave** looks at the plan and what you're taking: a rod with nothing it can fish, tackle for the wrong water, nothing that suits the fish you're after, the tent still in the garage when you're camping, a tent already pitched where you're going (no need to take it), the stove or cool box left behind. It never stops you going, and **Pack the essentials** fixes what it can from what you own.

**Driving** is real but small: the lanes run from home down to the coast path and along it to Willowhaven, Shell Strand and Cove Head, each ending in a car park with room to turn. **The road round the bay** forks off the drive at home: over the hill behind Willowhaven, up beside the Coldwater, east along the neck of land below Longmere to the **Mere Boathouse**, and down the old trail beside the Wend to **Wend Quay**: about a kilometre and a quarter of gravel, two minutes or so at the wheel, never steeper than one in four. The car won't go into the sea, up anything too steep or through trees, rocks and walls (it stops, and you can always back out), and **Back on the lane** rescues it if it's stuck. Driving yourself, the clock runs as it always does. You can also **let the drive go by** from the workbench or the driver's seat: a short fade, the car parked at the car park, you beside it, and the time the drive takes gone by (a few minutes along the lanes, half an hour or so round to the Mere Boathouse and Wend Quay). Heading home from the map with the car close by takes it home with you; without it, you make your way home and the car waits where it is (the map can take you back to it). Your boats and your camp always stay exactly where they are: a camp left pitched is there, with everything in it, when you come back.

**The boat trailer** stands beside the garage. Walk up to it with the car close by and **hitch it on**; it follows the car round (and the drive going by takes it too). A small boat (the kayak, the rowing boat, the skiff, the bass boat or the jet ski) comes out of the water onto it wherever the car can get close: bring her in near the trailer, step ashore, and at the trailer choose her. She rides on its bunks behind the car, and goes in again off it wherever there's water close behind (Willowhaven's slipway, the strand, Cove Head, Wend Quay, the Mere Boathouse): the kayak up the Wend's channels, say, or the bass boat down to the sea. A boat on the trailer isn't one of the three in the water, nor laid up (the harbour office says she's on your trailer). Back at home, unhitch it by the garage.

### Adventures

Small stories round the cove, each with a clue, somewhere to go, and a modest reward. Clues come from the Willowhaven notice board, or from a page you find lying about. Nothing is timed and nothing needs doing twice: if you've already done part of one before you find its clue, it's recognised and counted.

- **The Kittiwake**: an old photograph on the board. Find where she lies, and see her under the water.
- **Ada's notebook**: three damp pages from a local angler's notebook, in the punt, at the daymark's foot and in the strand's shelter, in any order. Each one tells you something true about that water.
- **Where the gulls dive**: get close to gulls working the water and catch one of what they're after. Afterwards, the gulls you've seen diving are marked on your map for a while.
- **A gap on the chandlery wall**: photograph a live cuttlefish and pin it up on the board. Your photograph hangs there from then on, and the chandlery gives you the striped deckchair.
- **The watcher on the Calf**: a card in faded ink about Tam Leask, who counted the seals on the Calf's skerry every morning for forty years. Land on the Calf, watch the seals, and find Tam's slate in the watch tower. It goes on the shelf at home.

Rewards are keepsakes (in the journal's Memories pages), map marks and that deckchair: nothing that changes how fish bite.

Press **P** (or **Take a photograph** in the backpack or the album on a phone) for photo mode: the interface steps aside, drag to look and scroll or pinch to zoom, and press again to take the picture. You see it straight away, can give it a title, and keep or discard it. On a catch card, **Photograph** takes a picture of the fish in your hands. The game notes what's in each picture (the fish in view, the places, gulls diving).

The journal's **Memories** pages hold your album (48 photographs; download any one as a JPEG, or the whole album as a single web page), the adventures you've come across and their keepsakes, your map marks, your camp, and the places you've found, each with what you've caught there, your best fish and the tackle that worked. Photographs are stored in your browser's own database, not in the save file, so the save stays small: a downloaded save or a cloud save keeps the list of your photographs but not the pictures, so download the album if you want to keep them elsewhere.

On the map, **Mark a spot** (or **Mark where you are**) adds a mark with a title, a note, and a star for favourites; the Memories pages list what you've caught near each one.

Camps you've found are listed beside the map: **Go there** takes you to any of them in a short fade (a little time passes, more for further camps), and **Go there** by your boat takes you back aboard. Your boats stay where you left them.

### Seven ways to fish

The rod decides how you fish; what you tie on decides which fish come. (Strike times below are for Standard difficulty.)

- **Float fishing** (float rod, with a bait). A bait hangs under a float at the depth you choose (W/S). The float dips when a fish nibbles (no sound: just watch it) and goes under when it bites: a soft bell and **Bite — strike!** tell you, and you have about two and a half seconds. The alert means *strike now*, not that the fish is hooked.
- **Lure fishing** (light spinning rod, spinning rod, coastal rod or offshore rod, with a lure). Hold Space to reel at your chosen speed (W/S: slow, steady or fast), tap to twitch, let go to pause. Each lure moves its own way: crankbaits float up when you stop and dive as you reel, spinners flash and sink, a jerkbait hangs still between twitches, a soft shad swims, a weedless creature bait crawls and hops along the bottom, a popper and a stickbait splash and walk across the top, a metal sinks fast and flutters. Predators **follow** a lure, and a twitch or a pause often makes them strike; pike lie still in the weed and **ambush** it. If you're reeling when it hits, it often hooks itself.
- **Carp fishing** (carp rod, with a bait). The rig sinks and sits on the bottom while the rod rests on a bite alarm. A single soft **bleep** means something is moving the line; a run means a carp has picked up the bait, and with the **hair rig** or **method feeder** it hooks itself: a three-note bell and **Fish on!** tell you it's already hooked. Press **G** to throw loose bait around the rig.
- **Sea bottom fishing** (boat & bottom rod, with lugworm, ragworm, crab or mackerel strip). A flapper rig or running ledger on the seabed. Watch the rod tip: little rattles are fish mouthing the bait; when it **pulls round**, strike. Flatfish (flounder in the estuary, plaice on the sand round Gannet Isle), wrasse by the rocks and sea bass take baits on the bottom.
- **Fly fishing** (fly rod, with a fly). Hold Space to false-cast: each stroke shoots more line out; let go on a forward stroke to deliver. A dry fly floats and drifts with the current, a nymph drifts under an indicator, a streamer is stripped back in pulls. Trout won't take a **dragging** fly: cast upstream of them and let it drift down.
- **Micro-fishing** (micro float rod, with worm pieces or maggots under a micro float on a size 18 hook; or a 1 g micro jig on the light spinning rod). For the small fish most anglers never notice: rock gobies, shannies and little corkwing wrasse at the foot of the Willowhaven quay and on the shallow weedy rocks round the Skerries, the Calf and the cove; shannies and black gobies at Wend Quay and in the estuary's eelgrass; minnow shoals in the pools of the Fernbeck and the Coldwater's dale. Swing the bait out a few metres to the foot of the wall and set the float so it just touches the bottom (W/S). A goby notices it, edges up, pecks (the micro float's pink tip barely twitches), and then drags it under: strike. They fight like fish ten times their size for a few seconds, darting for their crevice, and swing in on the line. Every one is a new name for the journal and a close look worth taking (E on the catch card), and every one goes back. Mind the size of the hook: a goby pecks a worm off a big sea hook without ever taking it in. A big fish mostly ignores a scrap that small, and the odd bass or mullet that does take it snaps 2 lb line almost at once. **Micro Fishing** in the backpack's ready-made setups packs it all.
- **Live bait** (`src/livebait.js`). A small fish caught on the micro rod or a sabiki (a goby, a shanny, a sand smelt, a sprat, a joey mackerel, a minnow or a small roach or rudd) can go **into the bait bucket** from the catch card (Q, or LB on a controller), and a prawn netted in a rockpool too (*Keep it for bait*, Q). The bucket holds six; aboard a boat with a livewell, the livewell holds twelve. Tie one on with Q (live baits come round with the baits in your backpack while there's one in the bucket; the gear chip says how many are left) and fish it under a float or on the bottom: a **live fish** is the best bait there is for bass, pollack, pike, perch, john dory and the big hunters, and a **live prawn** beats a dead one for anything that takes prawns; the grazers and the carp ignore both. A fish that takes it has it: one fewer in the bucket. The bucket's water goes stale over the hours (quicker on a warm day, slower with fewer in it), and in stale water they start to die, the delicate sprats and smelt first, the tough gobies and shannies last: **Change the bait bucket's water** (E) at the water's edge or aboard. A livewell's pump keeps it sweet.
- **Squid jigging** (squid rod, with an egi jig: 1.8 sinks slowly for the shallows, 2.5 for most places, 3.0 sinks fast for deep water or current). Let the jig sink to near the bottom, **tap** to dart it up, then **pause**: squid follow a darting jig and grab it as it glides back down. A touch on the line is a squid feeling the jig; let it sink on and strike when it takes. Squid gather where light falls on the water after dark (the lamps at the jetty and at Puffin Cove, or your boat's lantern) and hunt by day too, more warily. A hooked squid jets backwards in pulses and may squirt ink when you land it. In the journal and on the catch card they're measured by mantle length, and listed under *Not fish*.

### Rockpools at low water

On three stretches of rocky shore (east of Willowhaven, in Willow Bay by the cottage, and at Puffin Cove) the ebb leaves little pools in the rock full of sea. Walk out to one at low water and **Look in the rockpool** (E) comes up: the view drops to crouch over it.
- **What's in it** is listed and goes in your naturalist's notes as you see it: beadlet and snakelocks anemones on the sides, limpets and periwinkles on the rock, rock pool prawns flicking about the weed.
- **Turn a stone** (E, three to a pool) for what hides under it: a green shore crab, a cushion star, a rock goby or a shanny. Leaving, you put the stones back as you found them.
- **Sweep the net** (Space) for a prawn, a crab or a fish: a fish in the net gets a close look (its model, how to tell it, the look-alikes) before it slips back in; whatever you net shelters for a few hours after. A netted prawn (or a goby or shanny) can go in your bait bucket instead (**Keep it for bait**, Q): see live bait, above.
- **Photograph** (P) the pool: the card names everything in the picture.

The pools lie at different heights on the shore: the high ones are out most of the day and hold little but limpets; the low ones come out only near the bottom of the tide, and at springs, and hold the most. The tide comes back over them (the conditions panel and the pool view say when); a covered pool's prompt says so. On a controller: **A** turns a stone, **X** sweeps the net, **Y** photographs, **B** leaves.

### Diving: freediving with a speargun, and scuba

Stop over water a couple of metres deep or more (in a boat, or standing on a jetty, the rocks or a steep beach) and **Go diving** comes up on the prompt. Choose:

- **Freediving, with the speargun**: one breath. The gauge shows it running down: about 75 seconds lying still, much less swimming, less again kicking hard. The mark on the gauge is when to turn for the surface from where you are; at the surface you breathe up (about half a minute from empty) before the next dive. Run your breath right out and you black out and come to at the surface, with a word on why freedivers never dive alone.
- **Scuba, with the camera**: a tank of air, filled at the chandlery in Willowhaven. The deeper you go, the faster it goes (head up at 50 bar). The dive computer warns you if you come up too fast, and after a dive below 10 m it asks for a safety stop between 3 and 6 m. Take photographs of the fish (they go in your album, and the field guide marks them as seen). No speargun on scuba: spearfishing with breathing apparatus is banned in France and Spain.

You swim where you look: **W/S** forward and back, **A/D** turn, drag to look all round (hold the **right mouse button**, or **LT** on a controller, to look about without changing the way you're swimming; let go and the view swings back behind you), **E** up and **Q** down, hold **Shift** to kick hard (faster, but it costs breath and scares fish). **Space** fires the speargun or takes a photograph; **C** switches between the view through your mask and the view just behind you. On a controller: the left stick swims, the right stick looks, **A**/**RB** up, **LB** down, **RT** shoots. On a phone: the stick swims, drag to look, ▲ ▼ up and down, and the round button shoots. At the surface by a boat, a jetty or a beach, the prompt climbs you out.

**The reef and the kelp.** At Skerries Reef, look along the foot of the boulders: edible crabs and velvet swimming crabs wedged in the crevices by day, a lobster under the bigger ones, and on the shallow rock tops crusts of acorn barnacles and clumps of blue mussels; richest in the shelter of the arch, barer on the exposed boulders out at the edge. Fish go round the rock, the arch and the Hound rather than through them. Down among the stalks of the Kelp Forest the light comes through green-brown and dim under the canopy, and brightens again in its clearing and out on the open sand.

**Spearfishing.** Fish swim off if you rush at them (a wary kind sooner, and kicking hard sooner still); drift past slowly and they let you close. Settle near the bottom and keep still, and the curious ones (the sea breams, wrasse, bass, pollack) come over and circle you to look: the old way, waiting rather than chasing. The sights show what's in front of you (its name once you've caught or seen one, and roughly how long it is) and turn green when a shot would hit. The shaft is sure at about three metres. Aim just behind the head: a clean shot holds the fish; a tail shot can tear free. The gun won't fire at a fish you shouldn't take: never in fresh water (banned), never a protected fish (the eel), never bluefin tuna, and small fish (the smallest fifth of the sizes each kind grows to here) are let grow. Speared fish go in your creel to cook at camp, and in the journal as *Spearfishing*, so take only what you'll eat.

**The Kittiwake's bell.** A card on the notice board: her bell lies in the sand by her bow, nine metres down. Dive to her (on one breath, or a tank), photograph the bell and leave it where it lies.

**Dive sites.** Five places on the home coast are worth going down at, each different, each with a landmark to find and fish of its own (they go on the map as a red diver-down flag when you pass over them, with a tick once you've dived them; the yellow buoys show you where):

| Site | Depth | What's down there |
|---|---|---|

And five more at Las Brasas, in water clear enough to see the whole reef from its top (see [Las Brasas](#las-brasas-the-crossing-south)): **El Bajón** (3–20 m), **Los Arcos** (4–16 m), **El Veril** (20–40 m), **El Sebadal** (8–14 m) and **Las Coladas** (4–14 m), each with the life it's known for at its depths.

You can't swim through the rock (the Hound, the arch's piers, the boulders), but you can swim under the arch. The first time you're down at a site a note tells you what it is, and how far you can see today. **Visibility** is on the dive computer: about nine metres at home on a good day, four or five after a blow has stirred the water up, less in rain or after dark, twenty and more in Las Brasas' blue water. **Something to find** at every site at home: swim up close to it and look, and it goes in your log with a few words: on the Kittiwake six things (her bell, her boiler, her propeller, the steam capstan, her name and the bottles from her galley), the arch on Skerries Reef, the boulder in the kelp's clearing, the old anchor on Anchor Sands, and the Hound's crown and its Pup. Your journal keeps how often you've dived each site, the deepest you've been there, the clearest water you've had, and what you've found there (and whether there's more). And wherever you dive, the seabed is never bare: shells and scallops, starfish and brittlestars, hermit crabs, worm casts, burrowing anemones and sea pens deeper down, urchins and anemones on the rock, stones and mussels in fresh water.

### Bite alerts

Alerts are gentle and never repeat: a nibble on a float is silent, a strike-worthy bite rings a soft bell once (**Bite — strike!**), a carp or a lure fish that has hooked itself rings a three-note bell (**Fish on!**). The bite-alert bell has its own volume in Settings, and the master mute silences it like everything else. The camera never jumps on a bite or a hooked fish.

### Reading the fish

Every fish in the water is an individual: its length, and its own build (lean, in good condition, or fat and deep-bodied for its length, so two fish of the same length can weigh quite differently), how much fight it has in it, and whether it's bold or shy. The catch card gives its own weight and condition.

Each kind answers the water in its own way: trout, grayling and sea bass hold in a current that carp, tench and mullet keep out of; bass hunt in the surf when it's rough; most fish go deeper and shyer under a bright midday sun and feed better at dawn, dusk or under cloud; a storm puts most of them down; and splashes, a motor, or casting at the same spot over and over leave the water wary for a minute or two.

You can watch a fish make up its mind: it notices the rig (*Something is curious…*), follows a lure or rises under a fly, looks it over, and then takes it or turns away. When it turns away the game says why, in a way you can do something about: *the lure's too big for this one*, *it saw the line*, *too fast for it: slow the retrieve*, *the bait's too far above it: fish it deeper*, *the fly was dragging*, *this water's been disturbed lately*. The field guide's *Why aren't they biting?* uses the same reasons.

How a fish takes depends on what you offer and how you work it: a float bait goes under, a carp runs with a boilie, a trout sips a dry fly, a predator slams a lure that's moving well; a lure crawled slowly, twitched or paused often gets just a **tap** (a fish nipping at its tail): strike at once, it's gone in a moment.

### Playing a fish

A hooked fish rests and runs in turns. When it rests, hold Space to wind it in. Just before each run it flicks on the surface: **ease off** for the first moment of the run (the *surge*), then hold again to keep the rod up and the pressure on, which tires it. The outer ring by the rod shows how close it is; the inner ring is the line's tension, and the small dot on it is where your reel's **drag** starts to give line.

The flick is hard to miss: a splash and a sound where the fish is, the rod tip nods, the ring pulses amber and reads *flick!* (on a phone the button says *Easy*, and phones that can will buzz). The jolt of a surge builds over its first moment, so letting go as soon as you notice the run is usually still in time; holding on through it is not.

Each kind fights in its own way, and the prompt says what it's doing: carp, tench and wrasse plod off on long, heavy runs; trout, perch, mackerel, mullet and sea bass dart this way and that; pike and largemouth bass shake their heads at the surface; flatfish flatten themselves on the bottom; squid jet backwards in short pulses; tuna sound deep and circle below the boat; marlin, sailfish and spearfish greyhound away across the top, leaping as they go; sharks bore away deep, shaking their heads; a wahoo's first run strips line off the reel in a scream. Each fish fights as itself, too: a big one in fine condition runs longer and harder than a lean little one. On a run it turns now and then (a swirl where it swings round), and the jumpy kinds (trout, sea bass, garfish, bluefish, mackerel) **leap** clear of the water a moment into a run: keep winding, rod up, so the line stays tight. As it tires its runs get shorter (*tiring*), and at the end it rolls on its side (*beaten*): wind it in gently. A **thornback ray** kites off wide across the tide with its wings spread, then clamps itself flat on the sand (plating): a dead weight you pump up off the bottom a little at a time. A **catshark** writhes and rolls, wrapping itself in the line, in short jerky bursts, and soon tires.

**Every fish its own fight.** Beyond its runs, each kind has its own signature moves (`src/fight.js`), each with a tell you can see, hear and feel, and an answer with the rod and the reel. The meter's word and the line under it say what to do while it happens, and the first time a note explains it:

| Move | Who | What you see, hear and feel | The answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The charge** | trout, grayling, sunperch, barracuda, dorado | it turns and comes straight at you, a wake on the surface; the line falls slack, the rod straightens and the weight goes from the controller | wind as fast as you can (*wind!*): keeping up wins a lot of line; on a slack line it can shake the hook (not on Relaxed) |
| **The headshake** | sea bass, pike, largemouth bass, bluefish, meagre, sharks | up at the top, head thrown side to side: the rod tip knocks, water flies, a rattle of pulses in the controller | rod low and to one side (*rod low!*): it keeps the head down and the line steady. Rod up and winding, every shake snatches at the line; easing right off, it falls slack between shakes and can throw the hook |
| **The dive** | sea bass, carp, tench, pollack, wrasse, conger, grouper, amberjack | it bores for the bottom, the rocks or the weed: the rod slams down and it goes deep | side strain against it (*side strain!*) turns its head (*turned it!*); near rock or weed a dive reaches them sooner if you don't |
| **The last dash** | sea bass, carp, tench, pollack, wrasse | one more dive at your feet when you thought it was done | let the drag work, and turn it again |
| **The long run** | tuna, albacore, bonito, wahoo, marlin, sailfish | the drag screams and line pours off far past where it was hooked; the ring shows how much of the spool is left | let it go with the rod up. A drag far too light lets it empty the spool. On a boat with her engine running, the prompt offers to **follow the fish** (E, or A on a controller): she goes after it at a trolling pace and the line comes back onto the reel |
| **Sounding** | tuna and their kin, sharks, rays, amberjack | straight down and hanging there, a dead weight; the rod bent hard down | pump it up in strokes (*pump!*): hold the reel a moment (the rod lifts), let go (it drops as you wind down), again. Winding steadily barely moves it and lets it get its breath back. On Expert, lift the rod and wind down as you lower it |
| **The circles** | tuna and their kin, once tired | round and round below the boat, rising on the near side and sinking on the far; the rod tip rises and falls, a swell in the controller on each up-swing | wind as it comes up (*wind*), hold as it goes round the far side (*hold*): winding then only loads the line and loses ground |

So a big sea bass is a fight of headshakes at the top, crash-dives for the rocks and a last lunge at your feet; a trout is quick dashes, leaps and charges straight at you on fine line; a tuna is a long, screaming run, a dead weight far below, and slow circles under the boat. The rod is a spring: a surge slams it down past its curve and it rebounds.

**What you see of it** (`src/fightshow.js`), all of it by the size of the fish (a mackerel's leap is a flick, a sea bass's a crash, a marlin's a show): the take erupts at the top where a fish hits a lure or a fly near the surface, or shows as a bulge and a swirl when it takes down deep; a leap goes as high as a fish its size and kind really goes (most of its body clear, a marlin a couple of metres), the sea streaming off it on the way up, its head shaking and its gills rattling in the air, and back in with a crash: a crown of water thrown out low all round, rings spreading, white water, the sound of it, and on a big one close by, a jolt of the camera. A marlin, sailfish or spearfish stands on its tail and skitters across the top in the middle of a leap, and greyhounds on, a skip or two more after it's back in. A run along the top pushes up a V of water behind it, and a fast one throws spray off its back; a swirl and a flash where a fish turns near the surface. The line cuts the water where it goes in, throwing a thin sheet of spray as the fish swings it about, and lifts off the top in a line of drops when a surge snaps it tight. Close in and still full of fight, a fish thrashes at the top, the tail slapping the water (a last kick at the net, bigger for a bigger fish); landed, it comes out with a splash, and the water pours off the net as it comes up, or drips off a fish lifted out on the line. None of it changes the fight.

**Side pressure**: while a fish is running, swing the rod low to the side against it (A or D, or the stick across): it turns the fish and tires it sooner. A current running away from you helps a fish that runs with it; one running towards you helps you. A soft rod (the float and fly rods) bends further and cushions a surge, but tires a fish more slowly; a stiff one (the boat and offshore rods) the other way round.

**While a fish is on**, what to do now (*wind!*, *pump!*, *ease off!*, *let it run*) is written large at the top and centre of the screen, where you're looking, with what the fish is doing under it; the game's notes about the fight come up just below, and the ring by the rod shows the load on the line, how near you are to landing it, the line out and the spool. **The line and the reel** show you what's happening. The line runs from the reel up through the rod's rings to the tip and out to the fish: slack, it sags from the tip, lies on the water and drifts with the current (and bellies in the wind); under load it comes tight and straight, down into the water towards the fish. The rod bends towards the fish, deeper the harder it pulls, and springs back as the load comes off. When the fish takes line against the drag the spool turns and the drag clicks once for every tooth of its gear that goes past: a slow tick … tick as the fish leans on it, a rattle, then a buzz and a scream on a run, its pitch the speed of the line, climbing as the spool empties (a smaller spool turns faster), with the hiss of line tearing off through the rings on a fast one. Each reel sounds like itself: a fixed-spool reel's fine, bright clicker, a fly reel's coarse, loud ratchet, a multiplier's smoother lever drag; brighter and louder with the drag set tighter. Winding against a drag set far too light, it ticks over as the spool slips under the handle. The handle turns as you wind. Near the line's breaking strain the line glows orange and thrums, it **sings** (a thin rising whine), the tension ring flashes red and the meter says *ease off!*. Under the meter's word you see what the fish is doing and how much line is out. The reel and line have their own volume in Settings → Sound.

The drag is a clutch: when a fish pulls harder than the drag setting, the reel lets line go instead of the line breaking. W/S (or the stick) tightens or loosens it during a fight. A light drag is safe but slow; a firm drag tires fish faster but leaves less margin. Each rod starts with a sensible setting (light for fine lines), and the game remembers yours. **Line off the reel**: a fish that outpulls the drag swims off with line, as fast as it can swim against the drag's pull (nearly its full speed on a drag far too light, a slow creep against one that nearly holds it; a carp or a bass a couple of metres a second, a tuna several), out past where it was hooked if it keeps going, until it tires, turns, reaches a bank or the shallows (where it turns) or empties the spool. A drag far too light barely checks a run, so the runs go on longer, and winding against a resting fish on it only slips the spool; once it's beaten, rolling on its side, it comes in on any drag. Every metre it takes past where it was hooked has to be won back before it comes any closer, and the meter shows how much line is out and how much is left on the spool. (A carp on the carp rod with the drag right off takes 20–30 m past where it was hooked; on its sensible setting a few metres.)

When a line does break, a note says exactly why:

- **Tension**: you were winding hard just as the fish surged. Watch for the flick and ease off.
- **Drag**: the drag was locked, so it couldn't give line.
- **Snag**: the line was too light to stop the fish reaching weed or roots. Use heavier line for big fish near cover. Some kinds make for cover far harder than others: a pollack crash-dives for the kelp, a conger or a moray backs into its hole, a wrasse, a tench or a grouper heads straight for the rocks or the roots; a mackerel, a tuna or a garfish just runs, out in the open.
- **Rock**: the fish got down among the rocks and the line chafed through. **Braid** cuts on rock in a moment; a **fluorocarbon leader** stands up to it far better (wire and the heavy shock leaders better still), and side strain keeps a fish out of the rocks.
- **Teeth**: a pike cut line with no wire trace. The spinning rod has one.

On **Natural** and **Demanding** fish behaviour (below) a fish can also come off with the line whole: it **threw the hook** in a leap while the line hung slack (keep winding when it jumps), or a hook with only a light hold **pulled free** because the drag was set too tight for too long (a sensible drag never does it). On Relaxed, the default, a hooked fish never comes off this way. A **hake** has a soft mouth: a light hold most of the time, which tears free sooner under heavy pressure, so play it gently with the drag eased off. **The line's stretch** matters to the hook-set: mono (and to a lesser degree fluorocarbon) stretches, so a strike at long range sets the hook less firmly (a light hold more often, out past twenty metres or so); braid doesn't stretch, so it sets the hook home at any range, but it has no give to cushion a lunge, and a light hold tears free sooner on it.

**The landing net.** A fish light enough is swung straight in on the line (no more than a quarter of the line's breaking strain, and never more than about two kilos). A heavier one, beaten, rolls on its side alongside you: **net it** (let go of the reel and tap the cast key, or the button). The net comes out, slides under it, and lifts it in. On Natural and Demanding, keeping on winding to lift it out on the line can tear the hook out, and so can winding hard as it gives a last kick at the net; on Relaxed it's netted for you after a moment. Big fish drawn alongside the boat (tuna, sharks, billfish) are seen to there, as before.

**The moment it's yours** (`src/reveal.js`). Out of the water, you get a moment to look at it before the card comes: the camera swings round in front of you and in close to the fish held up in your hands (from a boat, from outside her, looking in across the side at you and your fish; in first person you hold it up to your eyes). The catch card then comes in beside it on a wide screen rather than over it. The weight comes up on the spring balance, the needle swinging past and settling, and as it settles you hear the news: a fanfare for a first of its kind, your biggest yet or **a fish of a lifetime** (within a tenth of the biggest its kind grows to), a chime for the rest, and a heave in the controller. A stamp says which, and under the weight, its place in your tally (*Your 4th european seabass · your best is 72 cm*).

Line strain builds up rather than snapping at once: when the line **creaks**, it's at its limit, and easing off right then saves it on Standard. A carp on the carp rod, played this way, is landed every time (about half a minute for a big one); a bluefin tuna on the offshore rod takes about forty seconds, a squid ten. The light float rod can land a carp too, with a light drag and patience, but a big one may reach the weed.

The right rod, a sensible drag and easing off at the surge land fish at every level.

### Fish behaviour

**How they swim.** Every fish curves its whole body into a turn, head and tail both towards the inside, as a real fish does. They don't all swim the same way:
- **Bass, bream, wrasse and the cod family** swim in bursts while cruising, a few strong beats of the tail, then a glide with the body straight.
- **Tunas, mackerels, the shoaling fish, eels, sharks and billfish** keep the tail going steadily.
- **Flatfish, rays and the cephalopods** ripple instead of bending.
- **A startled fish** throws a sharp C-shaped bend away from the fright before it bolts: the escape every fish makes.
- **A hooked fish** thrashes, its body whipping side to side as hard as it's pulling. A fish coming out of the water kicks, and one held up flexes now and then.

**Settings** → **Controls & comfort** → **Fish behaviour** decides how fussy the fish are, separately from the difficulty of the fight:

- **Relaxed** (the default): fish come readily and forgive a lot. The wrong depth, a hasty retrieve, a dragging fly or a visible line rarely put them off, the weather and the light matter less, and a hooked fish stays on.
- **Natural**: each kind as fussy as in life. The light, the current, the weather and a careless approach all count, and the fish tell you why they turn away. A jumping fish can throw the hook on a slack line.
- **Demanding**: wary fish. They spook at splashes and boats, see a thick line, and refuse anything not quite right; a hooked fish throws the hook on a slack line more often, and a light hold tears free under a drag set too tight.

Played carefully (easing off at the surge, winding through a leap, a sensible drag), every fish can still be landed at every level.

### Casting

Hold Space to load the rod: the ring on the water and the dotted arc of the cast move out at an even pace, so it's easy to stop where you want. Let go to cast. The rig flies along the arc you saw and lands on the ring: no dice, the same release always lands in the same place. Swing the aim with A/D (or by looking round) independently of where the boat or you face. Esc or right-click puts the cast down.

Two ways to cast (Settings → Controls & comfort → **Casting**):

- **Assisted** (the default): the ring is exactly where it lands, wind included. Hold as long as you like.
- **Manual**: the ring shows where you're aiming; the wind pushes light tackle off it (a fainter ring shows where it will really land, and moves with the gusts as you aim). The wind's speed and direction show while you aim; cast a little into it. When the rod is fully loaded the ring turns green for a moment: let go then for a few extra metres. Hold on past that and the rod overloads: the ring goes amber and the cast collapses short and to the right.

Rods and tackle matter: each rod has a casting weight range (shown in the backpack). Heavy rigs fly far and hardly drift; light lures, floats and flies blow about in the wind, more on a long cast and in a stronger wind (never at random: the same wind and release land the same place); a lure too light for the rod won't load it and falls short; one far too heavy isn't allowed at all (the backpack says why). Banks, high ground and overhanging branches stop a cast where they meet it, and a cast that would land on dry land or in water too shallow to fish drops into the last good water along its way. A note after each cast says how it went: *Right on the mark*, *4 m short: the rod overloaded*, *2 m wide right: the wind carried it*, *a branch caught it*. Bottom rigs and leads sink and settle; floats cock at the depth you set; jigs sink at their own rate. The fly rod has its own rhythm: false casts that shoot out a little more line each stroke, delivered on the forward stroke.

### Views and first person

The camera stays in the view you chose: casting, a bite and a hooked fish never switch it. It turns all the way round you or your boat (front, sides, behind), and tilts from high above right down past the waterline: below the surface the view becomes the underwater view by itself (the colour, the haze, the light coming down, the bubbles), looking up at the hull or your legs, and tilting back up brings it out again. Where the water's too shallow to dip into it skims along just above the ground or the surface instead, and it never passes through a hillside. **V** goes in or out of first person at any time (the rod in your hands, the line running out, the water in front of you; you can walk, row or drive in first person too). **C** steps through the views, including a fixed underwater view beneath the boat and one following your bait. **R** resets the camera behind you. Settings has camera sensitivity, inverted look and a **reduced motion** option that keeps the camera from swaying or turning by itself.

### The backpack

Press **B** (or the pack button). The backpack opens like your tackle, in three parts:

- **Equipment** (left): tabs for *Rods & reels*, *Lines & leaders*, *Floats & rigs*, *Lures*, *Baits*, *Flies & jigs* and *Setups*, each a tray of pictures. What you carry comes first; what's left in the tackle box is below it, marked *In the tackle box*. Filter by *Fits the rod in hand*, fresh or salt water, and carried or boxed, or search. Each tile says where the piece is: ✋ in hand, ● on your line, ◐ on your other rod, ✓ carried, ▣ in the box, ✕ no rod for it.
- **Selected** (middle): the piece large and well lit. Drag it (or focus it and use the arrow keys) to turn it; rods show the whole rod or a close-up of the handle and reel. Below: what it's for, its real numbers, whether it suits your rod (✓ fine, ! a caution, ✕ won't work, each with the reason: *"This 16 g lure fits your rod's 7–30 g casting range"*), the fish that take it (only the ones you've met are named), a comparison with what you have on now, and the action: **Tie on**, **Spool it on**, **Rig it**, **Take in hand**, **Pack it** or **Leave in the tackle box**. When an action can't happen here, it says why.
- **Your setup** (right): the rod in hand as it's rigged, rod → reel → line → leader or rig → bait or lure, with the other rod below. Click a part to change it. While you look at a replacement, the part it would change is outlined and the setup shows what the change does (weight, casting distance, line strength) before you commit. The creel and *Head back* are tucked underneath.

Line, rig and what's tied on can be changed anywhere; rods and tackle are swapped (and whole setups packed or saved) only within reach of a tackle box: at a camp, or a motorboat's locker. **Q** and **1**/**2** still work with the backpack open, **Esc** closes it, and Tab, the arrow keys, Enter and Space move around and press things without casting or moving you. On a narrow screen the three parts become tabs. Impossible combinations aren't allowed and the panel says why (a fly on a spinning rod, a 60 g stickbait on the light rod); combinations that work but badly get a gentle warning.

All the pictures are drawn by the game itself from the tackle data (`src/gearArt.js`), photographed once by a small studio renderer (`src/gearView.js`) and kept for the session.

**Ready-made setups** pack everything in one go wherever there's a tackle box or a boat locker: *Starter Carp*, *Float Fishing*, *Light Trout*, *Fly Fishing*, *Lake Predator*, *Coastal Spinning*, *Flatfish*, *Squid*, *Offshore* and *The Bay*. Each one lands the fish it's named for. You can also **save your own setups** (up to six) and switch back to them later.

Gear matters: a lure too big for a fish's mouth is ignored by small fish (so bigger lures pick out bigger fish); trout and mullet shy away from thick line; a big fish on light line runs further and can reach the weed; a pike's teeth cut anything but wire; flatfish only find a bait lying on the bottom.

**How they move.** Every boat is a real floating body (see *How the boats move* below): she floats as deep as her weight sinks her, rides each wave under her own hull, heels in a turn and a gust, leans on her keel, rudder or outboard leg to turn, carries her way when the power comes off, rocks in other boats' wakes and leaves her own, and bumps, scrapes and grounds against what's really there.

Walk up to a boat and press **E** to get in; step across between two boats side by side; step ashore at any bank, pier or jetty. In shallow water an outboard tips up and you can only creep. Fish hear an outboard (not the electric motors), so slow down near them.

**Anchoring, tying up and towing.** A second, smaller prompt above the main one (**Z**, **X** on a controller, or tap it) is always about holding the boat still, and says what it will do where you are:

- *Out on the water*, **Drop the anchor**: the anchor goes over the bow and down to the seabed on its rope, about two and a half times the depth paid out. She drifts back on the wind and tide until the rope comes tight, then lies there head to the anchor, swinging a little and riding the waves; nothing pins her in place. **Raise the anchor** hauls it in: the rope shortens, drawing her up over it, and it comes up to the foredeck (a few seconds; the motor and oars wait until it's up). It needs water at least a little deeper than she draws (or she'd swing onto the bottom) and no deeper than 25 m (the rope is 30 m): where you can't, the prompt says why. Under the water you can see the rope and the anchor lying on the bottom.
- *Beside a jetty or quay* (any of them: the old jetty, the landing, Wend Quay, the boathouse jetty, Puffin Cove, Willowhaven quay and its pontoon, the Calf's landing, and at Las Brasas, Puerto Brasa's quay and pontoon), slow and within a few metres of it, **Tie up alongside**: she's drawn in to lie along it, a line at the bow and the stern, fenders between, and stays there, the waves still moving her a little. Step ashore and use the quay; she waits. Tied up, the throttle and the oars don't move her; **Cast off** pushes her clear (out from the pier, or along it where it's shallow outside), and only into open water: if another boat is lying right beside her, it says so. Stepping ashore at a jetty makes her fast for you, and boats brought round to Willowhaven quay arrive tied up.
- *Towing*: alongside another boat, the main prompt offers to **step across** or **take her in tow**. A line from your stern to her bow: she follows where you go, swinging round behind you on the turns, and slows you a little. **Let go the tow** (the mooring prompt, or the same choice beside her) leaves her where she is, to anchor, tie up or come back for. A small boat can't tow a much bigger one: the kayak tows nothing, the rowing boat not the cabin boat. The towline is a 12 mm nylon line (`src/lines.js`): slack, it pulls nothing; taking the strain, it stretches a few centimetres and pulls the harder the more it stretches and the faster it's opening, and soaks up a snatch in its own stretch. The pull is on the throttle panel ("tow 4.7 kN, taking the strain"), and near what it holds the game says so. Taken up gently, it's a fraction of its strength; opened up flat out on a slack line, it comes close (the RIB snatching the centre console: 84 %). On Realistic the line has seen some seasons and holds four-fifths of a new one's load, so that snatch parts it: a crack, and she's adrift where she is. On Relaxed nothing parts.

Whether a boat is anchored (and where its anchor lies), tied up (and at which berth) or on a tow is kept with the save, so she's found exactly as you left her.

**The throttle.** The motor skiff, the bass boat, the cabin boat, the yacht, the centre console and the jet ski each have a throttle lever with notches (and the sailing boat's outboard a smaller one: R, N, 1, 2, F): **R** astern, **N** neutral, then **1** dead slow (about 1 m/s, for creeping round piers and moorings), **2** slow ahead (about 2 m/s, trolling speed), **3** half and **F** full ahead (on the bass boat the first two notches, **E1** and **E2**, are its quiet electric motor; the RIB and the centre console also have **C**, cruising, between half and full: on the plane at about 22 and 19 knots, easier on the fuel and the crew than flat out). The lever is on screen at the bottom right while you're at the helm (above the round button on a touch screen): click or tap a notch, or drag the handle; the fill beside it shows what the motor is actually giving. **W**/**S** (or the left stick pushed and let go, or **+**/**−**) move it a notch at a time, and it stays where you leave it; held down, it steps on, pausing at neutral before going astern. **X**, **B** on a controller or the big **Stop** under the lever knock it into neutral: the motor idles and the boat coasts on, slowing gradually. The motor takes a moment to build up and to ease off (an electric one less), and changing between ahead and astern it goes through neutral and pauses while the gear changes. Steering (A/D, the stick across) is separate and works at any speed, best with a little way on. On a controller the stick pushed mostly sideways only steers, so a hard turn never knocks the throttle up or down a notch.

**Or a smooth lever** (Settings → Controls & comfort → Throttle: *Smooth lever*), like a real boat's: push it forward or pull it back and it moves as long as you push (faster the further you push the stick), staying where you leave it, and the revs follow smoothly instead of clicking from notch to notch. As on a real outboard's control, the first part of its travel either side of neutral just puts her into gear at idle (dead slow); beyond that it opens the throttle, up to full at the end of its travel. The notches are still marked along it at the same places (trolling speed is where it always was), the reading says where it is (*ahead, idle*, *ahead, 48%*, *full ahead*, *astern, idle*), and the handle on screen glides with it (drag it to just where you want). Coming back through neutral it stops there with a click you feel: let go and push again to go on into astern. With a line out it goes no further than trolling speed, with a fish on no further than dead slow, as ever; **+**/**−** (or the D-pad with a line out) still jump a notch at a time.

**Walking about.** On the boats you stand in (the RIB, the centre console, the yacht), *Leave the helm: walk the deck* (the prompt, when she's slow or held still) lets you walk about her: the stick (or the movement keys) walks you the way it points from where the camera looks, round the console or the wheelhouse rather than through it, inside her tubes or gunwales and short of her very bow and stern, your arms swinging and your feet on her deck, so you go wherever she goes. She keeps on as she was (the throttle stays where you left it, the engine idles on). Cast from wherever you're standing: the rod, the cast, first person and the line all come from where you are. Back at the wheel, *Take the helm*. **With a line out**, on the bank or on one of those decks, **L3** (or Shift, or \`) lets you walk with it: along the bank to follow the drift or a fish (slowly with a fish on), or about the deck, the line paying out behind you or coming in as you walk towards it. The same again stands you to work the line, and walking stops when the line comes in. (The rowing boat, the skiff and the kayak are too small to walk about: you fish from where you sit.)

**Underway as real boats.** A fast boat climbing onto the plane rears up through the hump (when she's going about as fast as her own bow wave, her stern settles into the trough behind it), a few degrees on the RIB and the centre console and more on the skiff, then climbs over it and levels out. A seaway slows every motorboat: the resistance it adds grows with the square of the local wave height (sheltered bays count as calm) over her length, so the same sea slows a little boat more than a big one, and only once she's fast enough to slam (in a rough sea the RIB flat out makes about 29 knots instead of 35). A big outboard on a light planing hull builds its push over a few seconds, so the RIB takes about three seconds to get up to speed. The propeller's push has to make up for the hull resistance the game gives a boat, so it's bigger than a real propeller's; acting low under her stern, it used to twist a fast boat's bow up far harder than a real one does, and the RIB would stand on her tail and take off in a rough sea. Now its turning arm is scaled to a real propeller's (its sideways push, as it steers, still heels her on its full arm): in a rough sea, flat out, she's thrown about (her bow rising up to about 14°, a roll of up to 14°, a short hop off a crest now and then) but never on her tail. The tubes' tapered ends past the transom show from outside again (their faces had been drawn facing inward).

**Momentum.** Boats carry their way as real ones do. The game's hull resistance and the push that balances it are several times a real boat's (so every boat makes her real top speed and grips and turns as a real one), which used to have them leap up to speed and stop dead when the throttle came off: the nine-tonne yacht stopped from nineteen knots in three seconds. Now, once she's moving, whatever drives her along her track moves her as a boat of her real momentum would (`surge` on each hull; see `surgeShare` in `src/hydro.js`). The forces balance where they always did, so every throttle setting gives the speed it always did; only how quickly she gets there and how far she runs on change. Flat out, then back to neutral, in a calm:

| Boat | to 90% of top speed | runs on (to a crawl) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motor skiff | about 5 s | about 25 m |
| Bass boat | about 5 s | about 35 m |
| Cabin boat | about 10 s | about 40 m |
| Centre console | about 7 s | about 75 m |
| RIB (35 knots) | about 6 s | about 90 m: off the plane in a couple of seconds, then a long glide |
| Sport-fishing yacht | about 13 s | about 100 m |
| Jet ski | about 3.5 s | about 50 m |

So come in to a berth slowly, take the way off her early, or give her a touch astern (a propeller astern at speed doesn't do much, so a crash stop from flat out still takes forty to sixty metres). Below a walking pace nothing has changed: she answers the helm and the lever as she always did alongside a pier, at anchor and drifting, and the bottom, piers, other boats and ropes stop her as hard as ever.

**Engines that start like engines** (`src/engine.js`). Each motorboat has its own engine room: a big outboard with power trim and tilt on the RIB, the bass boat and the cabin boat, two of them on the centre console, twin diesels in the yacht, a pull-started tiller outboard on the skiff (and the sailing boat's little one), and a jet ski's start button. How much of it you do is up to you (Settings → Controls & comfort → **Engine start-up**):

- **Get in and go** (the default): she's running the moment you take the helm (you hear the starter turn her over and catch) and stops when you leave it, and she trims herself.
- **One-button start**: the engine key (**U**, or **L3** on a controller, or the prompt) runs the start-up for you one step at a time (battery switch on, kill cord on, into neutral, key on, glow plugs on the diesels, start), and another press stops her. You trim the leg yourself.
- **Full procedure**: the engine key opens the **helm panel**, which has every switch, the gauges (revs, battery, cooling, trim, fuel, gear) and the start-up checklist. A controller moves round it like any panel. Switch the battery on and clip on the kill cord. The starter only turns in neutral. Turn the key on and the alarm sounds its self-test; on the diesels, warm the glow plugs for a few seconds. Then start her: she turns over for a moment and catches, quickly when she's warm, longer cold, and a good while for a cold diesel without its preheat. Twin engines start one at a time, port and starboard. A pull-start takes two or three pulls from cold, never more. **Assisted start** on the panel does the same steps for you.

Once she's running she idles, the revs follow the throttle, the alternator charges the battery and water comes out of the telltale. The gauges beside the throttle lever show the revs and the trim. With the engine off the lever does nothing (the bass boat's electric notches run on their own battery, engine or no), and one of two engines running gives half her push. Unclipping the kill cord stops her, as it's meant to. With the tank dry she turns over but won't catch, and running dry she coughs and stalls. A flat battery only clicks, and is never the end of a trip: an outboard has its emergency starter rope under the cowling and the yacht a house battery to switch across to. With the cooling intake out of the water (the leg tilted up while she runs) she heats up and the overheat alarm beeps until she cools. Overheated, she runs on, but gently.

**Trim and tilt.** On the boats with power trim, hold **]** or **[** (or **RB** and **LB** at the helm on a controller) to trim the leg out or in; you hear the trim motor, feel it, and see the leg move. Trimmed in (down), the push lifts her stern and presses the bow down: best for getting up onto the plane. Trimmed out a few degrees once she's flying, the bow comes up, less of her is in the water and she's at her quickest (the RIB: 17.0 m/s flat out tucked in, 18.0 trimmed out). Too far out, the propeller starts drawing air and races, and she slows again. Beyond the trim range the leg tilts on up, but only at rest or near it: partway for creeping over the shallows, gently; right up, the propeller's out of the water. Or one press of **\\** (or the **Tilt up** button on the throttle panel) takes the leg, both legs on the centre console, right up by itself at rest or near it, knocking her into neutral; press again and it comes back down to run (holding the trim switch takes over at any time). Tilted, the leg swings up and aft, the propeller out of the water behind the transom, as a real one does. The skiff's tiller outboard only tilts. On Get in and go she trims herself, exactly as she always has, and the gauge says so. In gear with the leg tilted right up, the game tells you why nothing happens (and the LEG lamp on the dashboard lights). The leg stays where you put it, running or stopped, even with the tank dry.

**Revs under load, and the bottom** (`src/engine.js` loadRpm and propBite, `src/boat.js` propStrike). The revs follow what the propeller lets the engine turn: flat out from a standstill it labours at about seven-tenths of its rated revs (a lower-pitched propeller revs a little higher), and climbs to them as she reaches the speed the propeller's pitched for; laden, she never quite gets there (the RIB flat out with four aboard turns about 5,700 of her 6,000). When the propeller loses its grip (out of the water as she leaves a crest, trimmed so far out it draws air, or bent) the engine races. On the boats whose leg you trim yourself (any start-up but Get in and go), on Standard and Realistic, the leg stays down over the shallows: tilt it up into its shallow-water range, or creep, or the propeller finds the bottom. A tick when creeping; driven onto a bank, a bang and a bent propeller (Realistic, worse): she loses push, more the harder you drive her, the engine runs rough and you feel the judder. The Marine Works' Fit-out tab shows it, and a new propeller puts it right. On Relaxed, and whenever she trims herself, the leg kicks up over the shallows as it always has.

**Twin engines, apart** (the centre console and the yacht; `src/boat.js`, the levers in `src/main.js` and `src/lever.js`). Each engine drives its own propeller: with one stopped, the other pushes alone and pulls her round (the console on her port outboard alone does 8 m/s and swings 70 degrees off her course in twelve seconds unless you hold her with the helm). And the levers come apart: **;** (or **D-pad →** at a twin helm on a controller, or the **Both · Port · Stbd** buttons on the throttle panel) picks the lever the throttle works: both, the port one, the starboard one. One ahead and one astern turn her on the spot (the console, port slow ahead and starboard astern harder: round through 150 degrees in ten seconds, moving under a metre), the way twin-engined boats are brought alongside. The panel shows both: the one you're working, and the other one's handle beside it. Neutral (**X**, or Stop) joins them again, and so does picking both with them at the same notch. Apart, she doesn't ease one engine back for you when the helm's over at a crawl: that's yours to do.

**Work on the water** (`src/contracts.js`, `src/contractboard.js`). The notice board at Willowhaven has **Work** under its notices: jobs from the harbour's people and the bodies they speak for (Ruth at The Lobster Pot, Mags, Old Bert, the Angling Club, the Marine Trust, Wenna at the harbour office, the Sailing Club, and Paco by letter once you've been to Las Brasas). Each says what it's about, who it's for, what to do and where, what boat and gear to take, how hard and how long, what it pays, the standing it earns and whether friends can help. They're chosen from the world as it is that day: the fish that are busy (a species for the specials board, a big one to photograph and let go), dolphins near the harbour (a photograph of them riding your bow), whales about (one found and logged as it surfaces), birds working (a feeding frenzy watched close), a gale coming (no deliveries or racing that day, except tarpaulins to Wend Quay before it arrives), and fair weather and a boat that can make it (spares across the ocean to Puerto Brasa). Others: a mixed bag of sea fish on one trip, a trout on the fly, a sonar transect or temperature readings at the Trust's marks (some by night, and more pay with imaging sonar), a box of ice to Wend Quay, berthing at the visitors' pontoon without a bump, a course round the marks against the clock, and the same under sail. Take up to three: the next mark of one has a marker, all of them are on the map and in the journal (Memories). Out there, you do it with the game itself (the catch, the photograph, the finder, the sail); back at the board, **Collect** pays you, once (a delivery is paid on arrival). Give a job up and nothing's lost but the job; a course you're too slow round, or a berthing with a bump, starts again from the beginning; a job with a deadline that passes simply lapses. With a VHF radio the harbour office mentions work going on the board. The favours, the adventures and the badges carry on as ever.

**Cargo on deck** (`src/cargo.js`; the chandlery's *For the boat* tab). Things with a weight and a place in a boat you walk about (the RIB, the centre console, the yacht): a full **dive cylinder** (17 kg: swap your breathed tank for it at sea, for a second dive; Bert fills it again, free), a **fish box** with ice (9 kg), an **equipment case** (11 kg), a **spare battery** (24 kg: swap it in when a trolling motor's or an electric outboard's battery is flat; charged free at the chandlery), a **stores crate** of water and food (22 kg), and your **jerry can** (from your backpack's gear tab: *Put it aboard*; poured into her tank from the deck). Bought once at the chandlery and kept there until you put them aboard a boat lying at Willowhaven: each goes on her floor, **strapped down**. On her deck (off the helm) walk to a thing to unstrap it, pick it up, carry it, set it down, or strap it again. Strapped, its weight is hers where it sits; **loose**, it slides when she heels, lurches or turns harder than the deck's grip holds it (a cylinder lying fore-and-aft rolls across her at a couple of degrees of heel; a battery holds until she heels past about twenty-five degrees, or lurches as hard), fetches up against her side, her console or another piece with a knock, and its weight goes with it, so a heavy thing sliding to the low side heels her further. Only the boat you're in or near moves her loose things. They're kept in the save, where they slid to.

**Charters, freight and a career** (`src/vessels.js`, `src/passengers.js`). Each boat is good at something rather than better all round, and the board's bigger jobs say so. A boat has a number of **seats** (people besides you: one in the rowing boat, five in the RIB and the centre console, eight in the yacht), a **cargo** limit (from 120 kg in the rowing boat to a tonne and a half in the yacht), whether she has **shelter**, how much sea her passengers are happy in, and whether she has room and a ladder for **divers**. Charters: **whale watching** for up to eight, **dolphins on the bow** for a family, **divers** to the Kittiwake, the reefs and the Hound (the protected reef, wreck and sea fans have no anchoring: hold her on the spot with Spot-Lock or by hand while they're down, a minute and a half on the site), and a **fishing charter** for a few club anglers (their fish landed with them aboard). **Freight**: timber for Puffin Cove's jetty, 250 kg to 1.2 tonnes. Each offer names the boat to take (one of yours that will do, or the smallest to hire for the day at the Marine Works). The people wait at Willowhaven: stop in the harbour in a boat with the room and they come aboard (they'll move over to another of your boats there before the trip); try one without it and they say why ("room for 8: she seats 5"). Aboard, they **weigh**: 80 kg each, and the freight its weight, all of it in her payload and where it is, so she sits lower, trims to it and goes slower (the RIB flat out: 18.0 m/s empty, 15.8 m/s with four divers aboard), until they step ashore or the freight's unloaded. You see them sat in her, divers in their black suits. A long spell of rough water in an open boat (a minute of it, with the sea over what she's comfortable in) and they don't tip (the pay drops to 70%); the yacht's cabin keeps them happy in the same sea. Bring them home and collect at the board, once. Give it up mid-trip and they're gone, with their weight. A **career** stands at the top of the board: Odd-job hand, then **Boatman** after three jobs (a passenger ticket: charters of up to three), **Skipper** after eight (up to six, heavier freight) and **Master** after fifteen (the biggest charters and loads); the board only offers what you're trusted with.

**The Fifth Name** (`src/story.js`): a story with an ending, a few hours long, threaded through places and adventures the game already has. The Kittiwake went down on the reef in the February gale of 1931 with five aboard; the sea gave back four, and their names are on the stone by the harbour office. Finding her starts it (Old Bert tells you); her bell brings Wenna, the harbourmaster, and the name of the fifth, her grandmother's little brother Joe; Tam Leask's slate on the Calf has a note scratched on its back; the old keeper's log at Gannet Light says where a Spanish trawler took a half-drowned boy; across the ocean at Puerto Brasa, Chano knows the name of that trawler (his own boat is named for her) and gives you a letter that was never posted. Take it home to Wenna, and take her out to the Kittiwake's buoy at dusk (she sits in your boat with her sea pinks, and weighs in her) to lay flowers on the water; in the morning the fifth name is on the stone. One chapter at a time, in order; whatever you've already done counts when its turn comes (a card at a time, never a flood); the marker and the map show the next place for the chapters the adventures don't mark themselves; the journal (Memories) tells it so far; two keepsakes; kept in the save. The radio's calls hold off while Wenna's aboard.

**Research for the Marine Trust.** The Trust also posts work for its records: **a rockpool count** (walk out at low water and record four to six different kinds in the pools: each anemone, limpet, prawn, crab, goby and shanny you see counts once), **a seabird count** (hold your binoculars steady on birds until you know them, and name three or four kinds: dolphins don't count), and **a photograph for the Trust's field guide** of a named species (a snakelocks or a beadlet anemone in a pool, an edible or a velvet swimming crab on Skerries Reef, a corkwing wrasse or a rock goby, a kittiwake on Gannet Isle's cliffs): any photograph with it in counts, from the camera, the catch card or a dive.

**Calls, dive surveys, an expedition, and a start that isn't fishing.** The harbour office calls on the radio now and then (by day, afloat, in fair weather, a few hours into a game and every several hours after, never while the first-trip guide is running): **a paddleboarder** (or a kayaker who's lost a paddle, or a swimmer) carried out and hanging on to a pot buoy, **a buoy adrift** (a mooring buoy, a channel marker, a pot dan) that's a danger to boats coming in, or **a tow**: a little skiff with a dead engine, her owner drifting. She's a real boat out there: come alongside slowly, *Take her in tow* (the interact key), and she follows on your line under power (the RIB tows her at 9 m/s, the line tight); let her go and she drifts until you take her again; bring her into Willowhaven's harbour and her owner takes her from there. Say *On my way* and it's marked; you'll see them out there on the water. Come alongside slowly in a boat with room (the kayak has none) and they're aboard, sat in her and weighing in her; bring them home to Willowhaven and the harbour office pays there and then. Say *Not now* and nothing's lost. The Marine Trust's **dive surveys** ask you to dive a site and log a few things there you haven't found before (the finds you make diving count, as you make them). A Skipper is offered **the Gannet Isle season**, an expedition in stages: load 300 kg of the wardens' kit at Willowhaven (it weighs in her), land it at Puffin Cove, round Gannet Light, sound the Hound with the finder on, and home; each stage done the way its own kind of job is, saved as it goes. For someone new who'd rather not fish: the first-trip offer at home has a third answer (*Not fishing: boats and work*) that points to the board and the radio; Willowhaven's first tip and the board's own say there's plenty of work that isn't fishing; and until you've done a job, an easy first one tops the board every day (a parcel to The Landing, a few minutes' row along the shore, paid on the spot).

**Trolling motors and Spot-Lock** (`src/trolling.js`, `src/trollmount.js`). Willowhaven Marine Works fits an electric motor on the bow of the RIB, the bass boat (which comes with the basic one), the centre console, the skiff or the cabin boat (not the yacht, which is too big for one): a basic one for creeping about, a GPS one with Spot-Lock and heading hold, and a high-thrust GPS one for a heavier boat; a lithium battery bank doubles its running time. Each boat has an installation of her own, chosen from her shape: a base plate bolted on the foredeck to starboard of the bow cleat and fairlead, with the motor's deployment bracket and hinge, shaft lock, steering head, shaft, motor pod, propeller and its power cable, coiled down the shaft. The RIB's goes on a raised platform on two legs braced to her rigid deck, above her tube nose and clear of the tubes, the towing eye, the bow locker and the anchor; stowed, the motor lies along the platform on her starboard bow, and deployed it hangs outside the tube. On the centre console the bow rail's starboard forward bay is left open for it. Deploying is the real sequence, in stages that can be turned round at any point: the latch lets go, the motor slides forward on its ramps, the shaft swings up and over the bow to vertical, the lock drops in, the collar lowers it to depth and the head turns to face forward (and the other way round to stow, the head straightened first). The basic motor is swung down by hand and its depth set on the collar by hand; the GPS one has a lift-assist spring that takes most of its weight (still worked at the bow) and a remote for Spot-Lock from anywhere aboard; the high-thrust one deploys and stows itself and sets its depth by power trim, from its remote or the dashboard, its actuator drawing on the battery (flat, it won't move, and it's stowed by hand at the bow). **,** deploys or stows it and **/** sets its depth (shallow, normal or deep): on the full procedure a hand-worked motor needs you at the bow (walk forward on the RIB or the console, where a prompt offers it; on the smaller boats it's within reach of your seat; with a controller, the helm's prompt offers the helm or the motor), and on the simpler settings it's worked from anywhere. Its propeller turns only once it's locked down at a working depth; with the main engine in neutral the movement keys are its push (it stays where you leave it) and its head (straight again when you let go). On the GPS motors **.** holds her where she is: **Spot-Lock** notes the spot and pushes back towards it, harder the further she's drifted and the faster she's going, the head turning to push the right way, so she wanders a metre or two as it corrects and lies bow to the wind and tide on it. Creeping along on its push, **.** holds her heading instead. It's a real push from the propeller under her bow, wherever the motor actually is, not a frozen position: a strong wind or tide, a big sea (the propeller lifts out at the bow), a flat battery or too much boat for the motor and she drags off slowly, and the game says so. It runs on its own battery (charged at a jetty and overnight, kept in the save), and it stops when you put the main engine in gear, stow it, drop the anchor or tie up. You hear the latch, the slide on the ramps, the swing, the lock, the actuator's whine and, under water, its hum. On the centre console and the RIB it has a switch and a Spot-Lock button on the dashboard, and a LOCK lamp. Friends see your motor where it is, moving as yours does.

**The dashboard** (`src/dashboard.js`, `src/dashcontrol.js`). In first person at the helm of the RIB, the centre console or the yacht, the console is the helm panel: a red battery rotary, the ignition key (one per engine on the console and the yacht), the kill cord with its coiled lanyard (the yacht's diesels have glow plugs instead, with their own button and lamp), a toggle for the navigation lights (red and green sidelights and a white stern light, drawing on the battery with the engine off) and a horn button along the bottom; the chartplotter (speed, heading, the depth, your track; tap it for the fish finder's picture) and the gauges (revs, speed, fuel, battery volts, temperature, trim, and warning lamps for the battery, overheating, the kill cord, fuel and the leg, all lit for a moment as the key goes on) on stood-up screens above; and the throttle lever on its bracket at the side, with the trim rocker on its handle. Each moves when it's worked (the knob a quarter-turn, the key to on and over to start, the clip on and off its switch, the lever leaning ahead and astern, the rocker tipping), and the screens show the engine room as it is: there's only the one engine (`src/engine.js`), whichever way you work it.
- **Mouse**: point at a control and it's ringed, with its name and what it's doing; click to work it, holding the button for the key (on the full procedure, once for on and again to start), the trim rocker and the horn. Drag the throttle lever up or down, or scroll over it. Dragging anywhere else still looks round.
- **Touch**: tap a control (hold the key, the rocker or the horn).
- **Controller or keyboard**: the engine key (**L3**, or **U**) leans you in over the dashboard; the **D-pad** (or **Tab**) moves the ring round it, **A** (or **Enter**) works what's ringed, held where holding means something, and **B** (or **Esc**, or the engine key again) looks up. The stick still steers and works the throttle, and the bumpers still trim.
- **The start-up setting still applies**: on the full procedure every switch is yours; on the one-button start the key starts and stops her, and a ring on the dashboard goes to each switch as it's done for you; on get in and go she runs whenever you're at the helm.
- **The helm panel is still there**: the engine key opens it in third person, and Settings → Controls & comfort → **Helm: Panel** uses it in first person too. The bass boat, the cabin boat and the tiller boats are steered from a seat or the cabin's aft wall with no console in their models, so they keep the panel.

**Fishing under way.** You can cast from a moving boat at up to about trolling speed. Going faster, starting a cast eases the throttle back to slow ahead for you (a word says so), and you aim while she slows. The rig leaves the rod with the boat's way on it and lands carried along in the direction you're going (the aiming ring shows it, and the note after the cast says how far). With a line out the motor keeps running: the boat tows your lure behind it on the line that's out (trolling), and it swims as if you were reeling it, so fish chase it. With a line out the throttle goes no higher than slow ahead (+ and −, the D-pad, or the lever change it; W/S are for the lure), and when a fish takes, you knock the motor into neutral: the boat coasts while you play it, and dead slow is the most you can give it until it's landed. Passing back over your line leaves it slack; it comes tight again astern. The rowing boat and the kayak drift on the wind and tide with a line out, and the line tows the rig along the same way.

**The kayak's electric motor.** The kayak carries a little electric motor on its stern. **U** (**L3** on a controller, or **Lower motor** by the lever) lowers it into the water; then the throttle lever drives it (R, N, 1, 2, 3), up to about 1.6 m/s: slower than paddling (about 3.5 m/s), but silent and hands-free, for creeping up on fish or trolling a lure. Raise it again and you paddle. In very shallow water it kicks up out of harm's way. It runs on a battery of its own (shown in the hint), about a quarter of an hour flat out and far longer at dead slow; it charges while the kayak is tied up at a jetty or quay, and overnight. If it runs flat the motor comes up and you paddle on: the kayak is never stuck. Whether the motor is down, and the battery, keep with the save.

**Sailing.** The **sailing boat** is a little sloop with tan sails, tied up on your finger of Willowhaven's pontoon when she's in the water (or have her brought round to the quay). Raise the sail with **U** (L3 on a controller, or **Raise sail** on the panel); with it down she rows. Under sail she goes where the wind lets her: **W** hauls the mainsheet in and **S** eases it out (it stays where you leave it), **A**/**D** steer with the tiller, which only bites with way on. The panel at the bottom right shows her from above with the wind as she feels it (bold) and as it truly blows (faint), the no-go zone either side of it, where the boom lies, the sheet with a mark where it would draw best, her speed in knots and what the sail is doing: *luffing* (it flaps: haul in), *stalled* (sheeted too tight for the wind: ease out), *close-hauled*, *reaching*, *running*, or *in irons* (head to wind, nothing drives her: bear away). The sail itself shows it too, bellying out when it draws and flapping when it luffs, with telltales on its luff and a flag at the masthead. She's fastest across the wind (four or five knots in a good breeze, never much past six), slower close-hauled and dead downwind, and makes nothing within about forty degrees of the wind: to go upwind, zigzag, and **tack** through the wind with speed on (the boom swings across). Turning the stern through the wind is a **gybe**: the boom slams over, so haul the sheet in a little first. She heels to leeward, and top speed is had trimmed right and sailed upright, not hauled in and laid over. Trimming by hand, nobody eases the sheet for you: held in too hard in a strong gust she's **overpowered**, laid right over, and she **rounds up** into the wind, her rudder lifting and drawing air so the helm can't stop her, until the sail luffs and she stands up again (the panel says *overpowered: ease out*, and you feel her gripe). Ease the sheet in the gusts and she stands up and answers her helm. Trimmed right she stands up to a fresh breeze (about 30 km/h) on any point of sail; above that, close-hauled, you'll be easing in the puffs. **Wind lines**, white streaks drifting through the air with the wind (gusts and all), show where it's coming from and how hard while you sail; Settings → Weather & sea shows them always or never instead, and Settings → Controls & comfort has **Sail trim: Helped**, which trims the sheet for you whenever you leave it alone and eases her through the gusts, so she barely gripes (the Easy-going play style sets it). You can fish under sail: the sheet holds while the line is out, as a throttle does when trolling.

**The sailing boat's outboard.** For a calm (or to motor in and out of a harbour), the sailing boat carries a little outboard on a bracket on her quarter. **L** (or **Outboard down** on the sail panel; on a controller **L3** goes sail, outboard, oars in turn) lowers it, and the sail comes down; then the throttle lever drives her, up to about six knots. **L** again (or raising the sail) tilts it up out of the water. It runs on petrol from a small tank of its own (12 L), and a word tells you where the nearest pump is when it runs dry.

**Windsurfing.** The windsurf board lies on your finger of Willowhaven's pontoon, when it's in the water. You sail it standing up, side-on, hanging off the wishbone: **W** sheets the sail in, **S** eases it out, **A**/**D** steer (you lean the rig), and the sail panel shows how it's drawing, as for the sailing boat. In light air it ploughs along at a walking pace; from a good breeze (about 25 km/h) on a reach it climbs up onto the **plane** and flies, fifteen to twenty knots and more. Hang on to too much power for too long (hauled in hard in a gust, or a near-gale in the sail) and you're **catapulted** in: a splash, and a few seconds later you climb back on and pull the rig up. Ease out in the gusts and you hold it; Settings → Controls & comfort → **Sail trim: Helped** trims it for you, easing out past its best to spill the gusts. It has no anchor (tie up at a pontoon or a jetty, or step ashore), and no fishing from it. The trade wind at Las Brasas is made for it.

**The jet ski.** It lies beside the board. It drives on the throttle lever like the motorboats (a light touch goes a long way: half ahead is already eight metres a second), up to about seventeen metres a second, over thirty knots. It steers by its jet, so it only turns with the throttle on. At speed it leaves the wave crests and flies a moment before landing with a smack, and it throws a plume of spray behind it; in a calm it skims flat. It runs on petrol (60 L; the fuel point is on the pontoon). No fishing from it.

**Three in the water at a time.** You have eleven boats, but no more than three of them are in the water at once; the rest are laid up, each where she lives: the Willowhaven boats in the boat store behind the harbour office, the rowing boat, the skiff and the kayak pulled up above the tide at the camp, the bass boat in the boathouse on Longmere. At Willowhaven's **harbour office** (**E** at its hatch) you see which are in the water and where, put one back in the water (with three in already, you choose which is laid up in her place) or lay one up (she's fetched if you left her somewhere else). A Willowhaven boat goes back in the water tied up on your finger of the pontoon (see *The pontoons* below); the others at the camp's landing and the boathouse jetty. You can't lay up the boat you're in, one at Las Brasas (bring her home first), one on the supply ship or one on a towline, and one boat always stays in the water. A new game starts with the camp's three in the water; a game from before the boat store keeps the boat you were in and the two you'd most want, and says so when you start.

**Your boats in either sea.** At Willowhaven's harbour office, or Puerto Brasa's (the white kiosk on the quay by the gangway), you can have any of your boats in the water brought round to the pontoon or the quay; and one that's in the other sea goes on the **supply ship** overnight and is tied up at the pontoon here at eight the next morning (sleep, or rest a while). The bass boat stays on Longmere.

The yacht carries the board and the jet ski on her deck on the fast passage, if they're beside her when she sets off: they come off at Las Brasas beside her, and go back to their berths when she brings them home.

**The pontoons.** Willowhaven and Puerto Brasa each have a floating pontoon: a gangway of steps down from the quay, a walkway low on the water, and finger berths off it with room for the bigger boats. Willowhaven's runs well out into the harbour, with three rows of fingers either side; the second row's east finger (on your left walking out) is **yours**, with a plate at its root with your name on it, and your Willowhaven boats in the water lie alongside it, either side; the outer row is the **visitors' pontoon**, signed for boats calling in. The harbour's own boats go out round the end of the fingers, clear of the berths. Tie up alongside a finger as at any quay (**Z**), and walk ashore up the gangway. Willowhaven's has a **fuel point** near its head; Puerto Brasa's pump is on the quay at the gangway's top, and reaches boats at the nearest fingers and alongside the quay.

**The sport-fishing yacht.** She lies on your finger of Willowhaven's pontoon when she's in the water (or have her brought round). Beside her, **E** offers the helm, the cabin or the crossing. Two inboard diesels drive her (two propellers and two rudders under her stern, a deep twin throb), both on the one throttle lever like the other motorboats. You stand at the helm, to starboard under the hardtop, the wheel turning as you steer: quick once she's going (about twenty knots), slow to turn at speed and slow to stop, so give her room near the pontoon; slow, her twin screws bring her round handily. Her cabin works like the cabin boat's (below), with standing headroom, bunks and a galley; after dark a white light shows at her masthead. Above the helm, a rack of big-game rods and an outrigger either side; aft, a wide-open cockpit with a fighting chair, rods in the gunwale holders and a tuna door in the transom.

**Big-game fishing.** The great fish of the open ocean are trolled for: rig the **big-game trolling rod** (the *Big Game* setup packs it, with skirted lures and rigged ballyhoo), cast the lure out astern, and motor on. The yacht and the centre console have a **trolling notch** on the throttle (**T**, about seven knots), allowed with a line out; tow a skirted lure there for marlin, wahoo, dorado and tuna, or a ballyhoo at dead slow or slow ahead for sailfish, spearfish and sharks. Out in the blue water (the Blue Water off Willow Bay; past El Veril at Las Brasas) the big fish are raised by the wake from far off and come up behind the lure to look; when one hits, it hooks itself against the boat's way and the reel screams. The engines go into neutral, and on the yacht you fight it from the **fighting chair**, which swivels to follow the fish. Big-game fights are the longest in the game (a big marlin takes about a minute of careful play on Standard). A fish that size isn't lifted aboard: it lies alongside at the surface for a look and a photograph, and then you keep it or let it go. The shortfin mako and the smooth hammerhead always go back (no boat may land either in these waters); a marlin, a sailfish, a blue shark or a bluefin can be kept, for the table or to sell (a whole bluefin, brought in fresh, fetches the best price on the coast at **Kaigan**, see *The harbour*).

**Fishing standing up.** In the yacht and the centre console you don't sit to fish: cast as from any boat (aim where you look) and you step back into the cockpit and **turn to face your line**, whichever way it goes, straight off the stern as readily as either side, and you follow a fish round as it runs; a fish you let go goes back over the rail you're facing. When the line's in, you go back to the wheel. (In the other boats the rod comes round no further than your side.)

**The centre console.** It lies on your finger of Willowhaven's pontoon when it's in the water. Twin outboards, about twenty-seven knots flat out, and open all round: you drive it standing at the console under the T-top, leaning on the post behind you. Fishing, you step back into the cockpit, or, if you're facing forward as you cast, up into the bow. It runs on petrol (200 L; the fuel point is on the pontoon).

**The RIB.** A rigid inflatable, a fisherman's favourite, built after the aluminium-hulled fishing RIBs (Highfield's Patrol and Sport ranges were the reference: about 52–54 cm tubes, a 2.5 m beam with only about 1.5 m of room inside, a 24–26° deep-V): a powder-coated aluminium hull, deep-V to a hard chine, with a keel guard; on it, sitting right down on its edge, the tubes, which are her sides: half a metre across, grey, a few centimetres off the water at the stern and sweeping up to a pointed nose with a towing eye, their ends running out past the transom as tapered cones, a broad black rubbing strake round them low down and a grab line along the top on D-ring patches. The deck is sunk inside them (you sit on the tubes): grey anti-slip decking, a bow locker seat with a cushion, a stand-up console with a tall raked windscreen and a stainless rail round it, a jockey seat behind it to lean on or straddle, and a stainless roll bar over the stern with the all-round light, a whip aerial and rods in its holders. No fenders: the tubes are her fenders. She lies on your finger of Willowhaven's pontoon when she's in the water (a game from before her keeps her laid up until you put her in). The tubes are real in the physics: lying still they just touch the water at the stern, and they press on it as she heels or pitches into a sea, so she's the steadiest boat in a beam sea, held hard over flat out she banks into the turn and stays upright, and her landings are softer. She drives on the lever like the console (with a trolling notch), about thirty-five knots flat out, and you stand at the console and step back into the cockpit or forward by the bow seat to fish. Petrol, 150 L; the fuel point is on the pontoon.

**The cabin.** With the cabin boat (or the yacht) still and made fast (anchored, or tied up alongside a quay), **E** at the helm takes you **into the cabin**, and from the quay the prompt offers the helm or straight into the cabin. (Tied up alongside, you make her fast with the anchor as you go in.) Inside, the roof lifts away and the view looks down into the cabin; **V** looks round it through your own eyes, roof on. **W**/**S** (or the stick) walk between the doorway, the galley and the bunk; **E** at the galley cooks and eats, at the bunk sleeps (you wake in the bunk), at the doorway takes you back out on deck; **Esc** (or **B**) goes straight back out. After dark the cabin lamp is lit. You can't go in while she's under way, and a save made inside puts you back at the helm. Every boat in the water stays where you leave it, anchored or not, and boats reloaded from a save are checked: one that wouldn't float where it was goes back to its mooring. There's no damage to worry about, and fuel is light (below).

### Fuel

Only what has an engine needs it: the car, the motor skiff, the bass boat's outboard, the cabin boat, the sailing boat's little outboard, the centre console and the jet ski run on petrol (and the yacht's diesels on fuel from the same pumps), and the bass boat's and the kayak's electric motors each on a battery (and the skiff, with the electric outboard fitted, on its own). The rowing boat (and the kayak's paddle) never need anything. The hints show what's in the tank while you drive or motor (*fuel 31 of 45 L*), with a word when it's down to a fifth.

- **Pumps**: one beside the garage at home (the car and the jerry can), one at the root of Willowhaven quay (the boats alongside, and the car in the harbour car park), a fuel point on Willowhaven's pontoon (the boats at its fingers and alongside the quay), and one on Puerto Brasa quay at Las Brasas. Press **E** at a pump; filling up takes a few minutes. The farm's pump at home is free; the harbour's and the quay's are paid for by the litre, and the pump says what each fill will cost before you buy (40p a litre on the *Gentle* economy, £1.45 on *Authentic*; Settings → Controls & comfort → Economy). If your purse won't run to a full tank it offers as much as it will. With your purse empty and the tank nearly dry, Wenna sees you home on the harbour's fuel (five litres, once a day): money never stops you fishing.
- **The jerry can** (10 L) goes wherever you put it: the garage shelf, the car's boot or your backpack. Fill it at a pump and pour it into the car or a boat from your backpack's *Camping kit* section. Longmere has no pump, so the bass boat's outboard runs on what the can brings.
- **The battery** runs down while the electric motor runs (about ten minutes of it at full), and charges while the bass boat is tied up at a jetty or quay, and overnight whenever you sleep.
- **A full tank of the car** does any trip there and back; the workbench's packing list and the car's *Where to?* say what each drive takes, and warn if the tank won't do.
- **Running dry never strands you.** The car rolls to a stop: pour in the jerry can, or walk to a farm for a can (an hour). A drive you let go by with too little in the tank still happens, an hour later. A boat with a dry tank (or a flat battery) moves slowly under an oar, and from the prompt you can pour in the jerry can or have a tow to Willowhaven quay (or the boathouse, for the bass boat; at Las Brasas, in to Puerto Brasa's pontoon): an hour, and a few litres to go on with.

Boats ride the water you see: they heave, pitch and roll on the swell and chop, gently, and no wave tips one over. A kayak bobs on every wavelet; the long rowing boat and skiff bridge short chop; the broad bass boat sits steadiest. Unanchored, a boat drifts with the current and is blown along by the wind (the high-sided bass boat most, the low kayak least), so on a breezy day in the estuary you'll drift down the channel while you fish. At anchor it lies a few metres down-wind and down-current of the anchor, bow to its rope.

### Backpack, creel and camp

You carry **two rods and eight baits, lures, flies or jigs**, plus a **creel** for the fish you keep. It holds them **by weight**: 15 kg, which is thirty-odd mackerel, a few good bass, or one big fish on its own (an empty creel always takes one fish, however heavy: a yellowfin tuna fills it, and then there's no room for anything else). A bigger creel from the chandlery's counter holds 25 or 40 kg. Lists of what's in it go kind by kind ("Atlantic Mackerel ×30"). Nearly everything you catch can be kept, to eat at camp or to sell: of the 77 kinds you can catch, only the shortfin mako and the smooth hammerhead always go back (no boat may land either). A big bluefin fills even the largest creel on its own. Everything else waits in a **tackle box**: at camp, at Wend Quay, at the Mere Boathouse and at Puffin Cove, and in the lockers of the motor skiff and the bass boat. Open the backpack within reach of one to swap. Press **Q** to change tackle anywhere, and **1**/**2** to switch rods.

The top of the backpack has **Head back to: Camp (the tent) · The boat · Another camp (map)**. Use it any time to return to a safe spot beside a tent or to climb back into your boat, keeping everything you carry and have caught. If you ever get stuck on foot, a *Stuck?* prompt appears that opens it for you. (It waits while a fish is on the line.)

At camp you can light the fire, **grill** a fish from your creel (it takes half an hour of game time, and the fire must be lit), **eat** it, or have an oatcake from the tin. Hunger is gentle (with warmth, wet clothes and thirst: see [How you feel](#how-you-feel-warmth-wet-clothes-thirst-and-hunger)): you only get a little slower on your feet if you go a whole day without eating, and a cooked fish makes you **well fed**, which steadies your hands in a fight (the line tightens more slowly) for six hours. Sand smelt and anchovies are too small to keep one by one, a conger is far too big to bring aboard, and the protected eel is only ever watched.

### How you feel: warmth, wet clothes, thirst and hunger

Long days out leave a mark, gently. Your angler feels the weather and the trip, and a word or two by the clock says how you are (*Chilly*, *Damp*, *Thirsty*, *Warm*, *Peckish*, *Well fed*); nothing here ever stops you fishing or does you any harm.

- **Warmth**: the air is cooler at night, under cloud and rain, and higher up the fell (about six and a half degrees a kilometre), and warm at Las Brasas; the wind takes heat away, the sun gives it back, and hard work (walking fast, rowing) warms you. Your body follows how it feels over half an hour or so, so you warm up and cool down rather than jump. What you wear helps: the yellow **raincoat** keeps off the rain and most of the wind (but is hot in the sun), the **wool jumper** is warm even a little damp, and the **woolly hat** keeps your head warm on a cold night or up the fell (it replaces the sun hat). Put them on and take them off in the backpack (**B**), which also says how warm it feels to you just now and what would suit the weather.
- **Wet clothes**: rain soaks you (the raincoat keeps most of it off), and so do wading and spray thrown over a fast boat. Clothes dry in the air, faster in sun and wind, and quickly by a fire, indoors, in the car or in a cabin. Damp clothes feel cold, and look it (a shade darker).
- **Thirst** builds slowly over a long day, faster in the heat or hard at work. Your **water bottle** holds four drinks: **3** (or *Have a drink* in the backpack) takes one, and you see the bottle tipped up. Fill it at any camp or kitchen (home, the bothy, Bar El Muelle, a boat's galley), at the chandlery, or beside a clear stream or river (the prompt offers it); you wake with it filled, and a hot meal comes with a mug of tea.
- **Hunger** works as before (a fish from the grill, the oatcake tin, the kitchen), and now a few **rolls** in your backpack keep you going far from a kitchen: pack them (up to four) at a kitchen or the chandlery, eat one from the backpack.
- **You can see it**: cold, you shiver and hug your arms in while you stand about; hot, you wipe your brow now and then; the clothes you choose show on the figure, on foot and in the boat. A short hint says what would help the first time you start to feel something (not a nag: once in a while).
- **Gentle effects, only when a need is very low** (very cold or too hot, parched, hungry): a slightly slower walk (at most about a tenth slower, a little more when hungry), a little shake in the hands in a fight (its runs jolt the line a touch harder), and a little longer to get your breath back after a dive. Never more than that.
- **Your choice**: Settings → Controls & comfort → **Character needs**: *Gentle* (the above), *Shown only* (the words, the figure and the hints, no effects at all), or *Off* (the angler never feels the weather, hunger included).

### Reading the water

- **Rings** spreading on calm water: a fish rising to feed just below (trout rise to hatching flies on the stream).
- **Nervous water**, many small dimples together: a school of small fish near the surface.
- **A flash**: a fish turning in the light just under the surface.
- **A splash**: something jumped.
- **Fizzing bubbles** on the lake: carp grazing the bottom.
- **The sea boiling white**, out in the deep: tuna driving a sprat shoal to the surface.
- **Seabirds wheeling and diving**: a shoal of mackerel, sprat, sardines or anchovies just below, and often something hunting them (bonito, bluefish, bullet tuna, little tunny). Head for the birds.
- **Ripples and streaks running one way**: a current. The Wend runs out to sea down its estuary, and the tide races off Gannet Isle's points, through the Skerries and over Long Shoal, and fastest of all through Calf Sound, where the water stands up in white ridges; the bays and coves are slack.
- **Whitecaps** in the open: a fresh wind with room to raise a chop. Shelter in the bays and coves.

Each of these comes from a real fish at that spot. Press **C** to look underwater and you'll find it there.

**Fished hard.** Every fish you keep (rod or speargun) is remembered against the stretch of water it came from. Keep several from one spot and the fish still there turn wary, and the ones that move in to replace those taken are slow to come; you're told once when a spot has had enough. Leave it, and it recovers over the hours: a night's sleep or a day elsewhere and it's as good as ever. Released fish don't count against it (though a fish caught and put back is a little warier for a while). Nothing about it is saved: the sea starts each visit rested.

**The water's temperature.** The conditions panel and the fish finder give it. The sea at home follows the year a month or so behind the air, from about 12°C at the end of winter to about 21°C at the end of summer; in summer the sun-warmed top layer floats on colder water, so it's nearer 12-13°C below eight metres or so, while winter's gales mix it the same top to bottom. The lakes swing from about 3°C to 21°C, the rivers and streams run cooler in summer, the tarn colder still; the estuary sits between sea and river. Las Brasas stays between about 18.5°C and 23.5°C all year, and on the crossing it changes from one sea to the other. The fish already keep to their seasons by it; now you can read it. Each catch in your log keeps the temperature of the water it came from.

**Reading them right.** The signs you see are noted as you see them. Fish where one was, soon after, and catch the fish that made it, and you're told what it was: *"The birds were right: a shoal of Atlantic mackerel under them. Where gulls and gannets dive, bait has been driven to the top, and something is driving it."* The journal's **Notes** keep each sign: how often you've seen it, how often you've read it, and what you found under it.

**The water's colour.** Rain and a blow stir up the silt: the shallows go olive-green and you can't see the weed through them, and under the water it's murky. It settles over most of a day. The conditions panel says how it is (*clear*, *a little colour in it*, *coloured, after the weather*). It changes who's feeding: bass, conger and the flatfish hunt by smell and feel and come on in coloured water; the fish that hunt by sight (mackerel, garfish, wrasse, pollack, the breams, squid) do worse in it. The catch log notes it.

**The grounds.** The journal's **Notes** turn your own catches into local knowledge, place by place: what you've had there and, once there are three or more of a kind, when they came: *"Atlantic mackerel: 14 (best 38 cm), mostly on the flood, at dawn, in summer; most on sabiki feathers."* (the tide, the time of day, the season, coloured water, and the bait or lure that took most). Nothing you haven't found out yourself. The map's list of places says what you've had at each.

**Life in the open water.** Down in the water there's more than the fish: moon jellies and compass jellies drifting and pulsing through the warm months at home (none in winter), the odd mauve stinger at Las Brasas all year, and shoals of small fry (sand smelt over the shallow sand and weed at home, silversides over the island's reef) that swim together, all facing the same way, and turn away and dart off as one when you swim at them. And the reef's own small fish, the everyday life a diver swims among (none of them on anyone's hook): by what's below you, wrasse hanging over the rocks with two-banded bream and young pollack round them, salema grazing over the weed, black bream, grey mullet over the shallow sand, a gurnard on the bottom, and out in the open water shoals of bogue, sand smelt and, over deeper ground, horse mackerel; at Las Brasas clouds of chromis over the reef, ornate wrasse and parrotfish. Each shoal drifts slowly about its patch with all its fish facing the way it's going (a wrasse hovering by the rock faces its own way). Swim at them and they turn away together and move off, then drift back, coming round slowly the way they turned. It's the diver they watch, not the camera behind. The curious ones (wrasse, bream, chromis) come over to look at you if you hang still in the water for a moment. Fewer on lower graphics settings. At Las Brasas the seabed has its own life too: long-spined urchins in herds on the black rock, red sea stars and sponges, and on the sand sea cucumbers and fields of garden eels. All of it is in the field notes once you've met it.

**Field notes.** The naturalist's pages: the life of the seabed, the reef and the wreck you've met up close while diving (starfish and brittlestars, urchins, scallops and hermit crabs, lugworm casts, burrowing anemones and sea pens, dahlia, jewel and plumose anemones, dead man's fingers, pink sea fans, sponges, kelp and the red weeds under it, the lobster, and the swan mussels in the lakes), each with its Latin name, a line on how it lives, and where you first met it; and the sea life you've seen from the boat.

**Species: the tree of life.** The journal's **Species** pages (J, then *Species*) hold every living thing on the coast, 128 kinds in all, sorted as a zoologist would: kingdom, phylum, class, order and family. That's the fish you catch and the ones you only see, the whales, dolphins, seals and otters, the seabirds and the birds you only hear, the loggerhead turtle, frogs, and the small life of the seabed and the open water. It even includes the few things there that aren't animals: the kelp, the red weeds and the glowing plankton. On the left is the tree, each class with how many of its kinds you've recorded. Choose a class and the right page lists it by order and family. Each kind has its scientific name and its IUCN Red List status (LC, NT, VU, EN, CR, DD), and either what you've recorded of it (caught, with your best; seen; met on a dive; or heard, not yet seen) or where to look for it. A fish's card opens it in the field guide. Names and ranks follow WoRMS, the World Register of Marine Species. The orders of fishes follow Eschmeyer's Catalog of Fishes, which leaves the big perch-like branch's orders open while they're redrawn from DNA, as the pages do. The **herring gulls** round Gull Rock and over the bay count when you're near them by day. A **curlew** calling across the cove channel, a **tawny owl** hooting ashore at night and **frogs** croaking by the lakes count as heard, with the sound on.

**Birds of the coast, and the binoculars.** Some 170 birds of 29 more kinds live along the coast, each where it really would and doing what it really does:
- **Gannet Isle:** guillemots and razorbills on its rocky edge and rafted on the sea below, and puffins on the turf over Puffin Cove and on the water (all three in spring and summer only). Fulmars glide the island's edge on stiff wings, with a peregrine high over it.
- **Gull Rock:** kittiwakes on the stacks' ledges and wheeling round them, fulmars, and shags at the foot.
- **The Skerries and the Calf's skerry:** shags and great black-backed gulls on the rocks, eiders rafted beside them. Choughs tumble round the Calf, with ravens above.
- **Cove Head and Shell Strand:** a kestrel hovering over Cove Head, turnstones along Shell Strand's tideline, and a rock pipit in the rocks.
- **The Saltings:** redshanks, curlews and little egrets on the mud.
- **Rivers and lakes:** a kingfisher dashing low along the Fernbeck and the upper Wend, dippers on the stones, and mute swans and mallards on the lakes.
- **Out at sea:** Manx shearwaters skimming the waves, tilting black and white.
- **Las Brasas:** yellow-legged gulls round the harbour, an osprey over the west cliffs, a kestrel over the Roja, whimbrels and turnstones on the Faro point's rocks, little egrets, Berthelot's pipits and Atlantic canaries round the village, plain swifts screaming over it, and Bulwer's petrels low over the sea after dark.

### The harbour: the fish market, Kaigan and the inn

Willowhaven is a working harbour, and a catch you keep can do more than go on the grill.

- **The fish market**, the open shed by the notice board, at the head of the quay (open 5 am to 7 pm). Mags Pennock buys what's in your creel **by weight**: the dear fish (sole, turbot, bass) fetch more a kilo than the cheap ones (mackerel, scad, bogue), and the lakes' coarse fish nobody buys. Each day the slate beside the market chalks up two kinds that are **wanted** (half as much again) and one there's **plenty of** (a bit less). Fish fetch their best **fresh**, within half a day of the catch; then the price slips, to just over half after two days. (A camp's cool box keeps fish on ice.) The **seasons** move prices too: a fish in its season comes in by the boxful and fetches a little less, one out of season is scarce and fetches up to a fifth more (the sell list says which). A **prime** fish, near the top of its size, fetches more by the kilo (15%), a small one a little less. Every sale comes with **Mags's receipt**: each fish's weight, the price by the kilo and why (wanted, in season, prime, how fresh), and the total. No one fish pays more than £40, except the big ocean fish brought back from the blue water (a tuna, a wahoo, a dorado, an albacore): up to £120.
- **Kaigan**, in the old net store at the east end of the apron (tarred black boards still, nets drying beside it, but now a window either side of the door, an indigo noren over it and red paper lanterns that glow at night): a **Nikkei kitchen**, Japanese and Peruvian, serving sushi, sashimi, ceviche and tiradito. Kenji Higa, its chef, buys at the kitchen door from 10 am to 9 pm, and pays **well over the market** for the forty-odd sea fish his counter serves (bluefin and yellowfin, albacore and bonito, amberjack, wahoo and dorado, the billfish, bass, bream, turbot, sole, John Dory, mackerel, scad and sardines, squid, cuttlefish and octopus, conger for anago, meagre for the classic ceviche, grouper for tiradito). Only fish **fit to eat raw**: caught within **twelve hours** (time on ice doesn't count, so a bag of Bert's ice keeps the clock stopped); a day-old fish he takes for the hot kitchen at a little over half the price, older not at all. Never a fresh water fish raw, and never the risky ones (blue shark, barracuda, salema). Two **specials** a night on his board pay half as much again; a prime fish more by the kilo. A **whole bluefin** is the house's special whenever one comes in: he breaks it down at the counter himself, and pays by its weight, easing towards £500 for the biggest (a 150 cm fish, some 67 kg, fetches around £470, where Mags can give £120). Other fish ease towards £90, the big ocean fish towards £160 to £200, so a bigger fish always fetches more. Mags tells you, on her slab, when Kenji would give you more.
- **The Lobster Pot**, the whitewashed inn at the back of the apron, facing straight down the quay. **Walk in** through the door (it swings open as you come; or press **E** at it): low beams, flagstones, a coal fire with a high-backed settle, two tables by the windows, the bar with its pumps and a stuffed bass over it, and the kitchen behind its pass, with a chalkboard of what Ruth wants tonight. Inside, the roof and the wall nearest the camera lift away so you can see (in first person, they stay). Step up to the bar (**E**) for the bar's screen, to the pass for the kitchen's, or sit at the window table or on the settle by the fire a while: Ruth brings you a mug of tea. A quiet murmur of talk, a glass set down, the clock; the busier the bar, the louder. If Ruth's not behind the bar (first thing, a breather on the bench, late), ring the bell and she comes through. Ruth Alder runs the bar and the kitchen:
  - **The bar**: buy a pint (£4) and hear one of the harbour's stories (a new one each time, until you've heard them all); ask Ruth what's biting (worked out from the water as it is: what's about and busy round the harbour, the hour, the wind and rain, a storm); and hear **what they're saying**: every rumour is true, read off the world as it is (gannets diving on a shoal, the tuna up and the sea boiling, a pod of dolphins, a whale blowing, the sea set to glow tonight, a good fish you landed today).
  - **The kitchen**: two **orders** a day for particular fish (sometimes a good-sized one), paying well over the market (up to £60), until 10 pm. A **fine fish** (well over the size asked for) earns a quarter more. The kitchen **remembers its suppliers**: after three orders you're a regular supplier (a tenth over each order's price), after eight the kitchen's own supplier (a fifth over, and your name goes on the board with the dish). Or order the plate of the day (£9), or have Ruth cook a fish of yours (£3). Either way you're well fed.
- **The chandlery**, the green weatherboarded shop with the buoys on its wall. Walk in: Bert's counter at the back with the shelves of hooks, line and reels behind it, the rod rack and a pegboard of lures down the left wall (**E** there: the tackle, free as always), the bait freezer, the dive compressor by the window (**E** fills your tank), floats hanging from the beams. Ask Bert what's biting at the counter (the same reading of the water as the inn's). If he's not behind it (a fine morning in his chair outside, or after hours), ring the bell. **The chandlery's counter.** The tackle store is free to take from, as always (the first button). At the counter, Old Bert Hale sells:
  - **creels**: a bigger one (25 kg of fish, £30) and a cool-lined wicker one (40 kg, £80, and fish in it keep fresh twice as long);
  - **ice** (£2): fish in your creel keep fresh for a day (their freshness clock stops while they're on ice);
  - **fresh bait** (£3): ragworm, peeler crab and sandeel dug that morning; for the rest of the day fish find your natural baits from further off.
  - **Mending**: line wears in long, hard fights (the longer and harder, the more). Frayed line is a little weaker, never more than a fifth, and Bert puts fresh line on for £3 to £8.
  - **Ready for a trip**: a checklist of your creel space, ice, water bottle, rolls, lines, your boat's fuel, the weather and what's about, with a button to put right whatever needs it.
  - **Worth going after today**: Bert's tip, a fish the market or the kitchen wants that's in season now, where it lives, and the rod and bait he'd put on for it.
- **Bar El Muelle**, at Puerto Brasa, works the same way: Paco's bar and stories, his mother's kitchen with an order a day, and Paco buys the island's fish from the boats. Your room upstairs is a button away.

**The harbour's people** go about their days, by the clock, the weather and the fish. Walk up to one and press **E** to talk. They move like people: a walk paced to how fast they're going (no feet sliding), rising over each step, hips swaying and shoulders turning against them, arms swinging from the elbow, and a run for the morning jogger; stood about, they breathe and shift their weight, hands in pockets or behind their back or arms folded, each in their own way, and step round when they turn. Their work is their own (Ruth sweeping the step with a broom, Mags gutting at the slab, Dougie crouched over a pot with his netting needle, Ruth wiping the bar), and walking to it they carry what it needs: a rod and a bucket, a fish box, a pot. They notice you: a look your way as you pass, a wave the first time in a while, and stood about they turn to you. Two stood together pass the time of day, turn and turn about, hands going. **Passers-by** (`src/world/passersby.js`): of a fine day Willowhaven has more people about than its townsfolk: a man walking his dog (trotting at heel, off sniffing when he stops, sitting now and then), a couple from the inn strolling to look at the boats and the fish on the slab, the girl from the inn carrying fish boxes, a runner first thing, a child with a crab line at the slipway of a summer afternoon. Each comes out of a door at their time (most days; more in summer, fewer in winter), walks the harbour's paths from one place to the next, stops a while, steps round you (or waits, and squeezes by if you won't budge), and goes in at the end of their day or when it comes on to rain. **Your own figure** bends its arms at the elbow (holding the rod, drinking, warming your hands), sways as it walks, leans back into a cast and through it, and steps up and over a boat's side when you get in or step ashore.

- **Mags Pennock** keeps the fish market, behind her slab from five in the morning till seven at night, and some evenings goes over to the inn.
- **Old Bert Hale** stands behind his counter in shop hours, sits in his chair by the chandlery door on a fine morning and again in the late afternoon, and has his pint on the settle by the fire at the inn of an evening.
- **Ruth Alder** sweeps the front of The Lobster Pot first thing, works behind her bar from then till last orders (and calls time at the door), and takes five minutes on the bench in the afternoon if it's dry.
- **The regulars** come in through the door to the inn: Dougie stands at the bar for his lunch and again of an evening, Hettie has her lunch at the window table, Ian his pint at the other table after his afternoon out, and Mags some evenings.
- **Dougie Ferris** works lobster pots off Gull Rock from his boat, the green-and-red **Morning Star**. At first light he's down to the slipway and out to her on her mooring off the quay; she runs out round the Calf to his buoys (orange floats with a flag), stops to haul each pot, and comes in about noon to lie alongside the end of the quay while he lands his catch; then back to her mooring. In a storm, a gale or a downpour she stays in: Dougie mends pots on the apron, then fishes in the lee of the quay. If a blow comes on while he's out, he leaves the rest of his pots and comes straight in.
- **Hettie Moss**, the keenest angler on the coast, goes where the fish are: Shell Strand for the bass at dawn and dusk (she cycles over), the end of the quay when the mackerel or garfish are in, the slipway for flounder on a quiet day, squid under the quay light at night, and the sheltered water in the lee of the quay in a storm. Now and then you'll see her hold one up.
- **Kit** crabs off the side of the quay on a fine day, mornings and late afternoons.
- **Ian Rennie**, a retired schoolmaster, takes his skiff the **Bonny Jean** (cream and teal) off the other mooring by the quay on a calm, dry afternoon, out past the Calf to drift and feather for mackerel; back by six for a pint. On a fine morning he reads on the bench.
- **The Puffin**, a little cruising boat with a tan sail, comes in from the sea on some fine afternoons and anchors in the bay for the night (her light lit after dark), then sails on round the coast next morning. If it's blowing when she's due to go, she waits for it to pass.
- **Morwenna (Wenna) Penhallow**, the harbourmaster, keeps the harbour office: in at its hatch from eight (she looks up from her ledger when you come to see to your boats), down to the top of the gangway mid-morning and late afternoon to look over the lines on the pontoon, lunch at The Lobster Pot, and an hour on the bench of a dry evening; she lodges at the inn.
- **The marina's boats.** Locals' boats lie on their berths along the fingers, lines out and fenders down (the cabin boat *Bluebell*, the skiff *Tern*, the little sloop *Marigold*, the motor yacht *Lady Ellen* and the console boat *Osprey*); their owners are away, so they stay put, but they're solid, and the harbour's skippers steer round them as round yours. The **Sea Wren**, a charter boat, goes out most fair mornings at nine with a party: astern out of her berth along the first finger, away round the end of the fingers to drift for pollack off Gull Rock, and home bow first in the afternoon. Some fine afternoons the **Halcyon**, a motor yacht cruising the coast, comes in round the north of the Shell Strand shoals and lies alongside the visitors' row for the night, backing off and away next morning. And on a fine spring or summer afternoon two kayaks paddle out from the slipway round the west of the bay.
- **Paco** stands in the door of Bar El Muelle at Las Brasas.
- **Chano**, a fisherman of Puerto Brasa, takes his white-and-blue boat the **Virgen del Carmen** out of the harbour mouth at sunrise to El Bajón, handlines over the pinnacles by the yellow buoy, and comes back late in the morning to land his catch at the quay steps for Paco's kitchen. When the trade wind blows too hard he mends his nets on the quay instead. Lunchtime and evenings he sits at the white table outside the bar.

**At the helm.** The harbour's boats keep to their days by the clock, but they're skippered (`src/world/skipper.js`): each looks ahead and keeps clear of every other boat (yours lying on a mooring or alongside, yours under way, a friend's, each other's), steering round one in the way, stopping to let it by when there's no room, then catching up and back onto her line; and she comes in to her mooring or a berth without leaning on the boat beside her. They're solid, too: run into one and you bump off her, and she's shoved aside and swings back. (When the clock jumps on, after a night's sleep, they're simply where it puts them.)

**Favours.** Now and then, when you stop for a word, someone asks you a small favour: Mags needs a good sole for her mother's eightieth, Ruth a big bass for a wedding party, Dougie two mackerel for bait or his pot buoy found (it's adrift at sea: an orange float with a flag, and your map gets a mark near where); Bert wants a box of hooks taken to Hettie, or his pike record beaten; Hettie bets you can't catch a squid off the quay after dark; Kit longs to hear about a whale; Ian about the fin whale; Wenna about the grey seal that follows the boats in of an evening; Paco needs a big vieja; Chano would love a photograph of the old grouper in El Bajón's cave. One at a time from each of them, three at most on the go, nothing timed: say *Not now* and they'll ask again another day. Mags, Ruth, Bert and Paco ask across the counter (in the market, inn, chandlery and bar screens); the rest when you walk up and press **E**. What they give back: a little money, a keepsake for the shelf at home (Bert's pike plug, Hettie's lucky sandeel, Kit's drawing, Chano's hook), a mark on your map only a local would know, or supper. The journal lists them under **Memories → Favours**.

**They remember you.** The first time you stop for a word each day, they may mention the good fish you had yesterday, the trip you've just come back from, or the place you've just found.

What they're up to (Dougie out at his pots, or not going out; what he landed; Hettie's mark and her best fish of the day; Kit on the quay; Ian out after the mackerel; the Sea Wren out with a party; the Halcyon at the visitors' pontoon; kayaks in the bay; the Puffin coming in or at anchor) is part of the talk at The Lobster Pot, and Chano's morning (and his catch) at Bar El Muelle. There are no seasons or tides in the game yet, so their days follow the clock, the weather and the fish.

You start with £20 in your purse; sold fish show as *Sold* in the catch log. Every payment goes through one purse (`src/economy.js`): it's never overdrawn, nothing is half-paid, and the last forty payments in and out are kept as receipts. The chandlery's tackle stays free. Money buys small pleasures at the inn and the counter, pays for fuel at the harbour pumps, and buys boats at the Marine Works. It never buys a better bite.

**Goals** (the journal's Days page, under your record; `src/goals.js`): every kind of fish (and every fresh-water and every sea fish, counted apart), a fish of a lifetime (within a tenth of the biggest its kind grows to), boats of your own, every place on the coast found, and across the ocean to Las Brasas. Each shows how far along you are, ticked off as you reach it.

### Seasons

The year turns: spring, summer, autumn and winter, a week of days each (a short year, so the seasons come round while you play). A new game starts in mid-spring, at eight in the morning, so your first trip is in daylight. The clock shows the season beside the time of day, the notice board gives the day's sunrise and sunset, and a word marks each season's turn.

- **The light**: at midsummer the sun's up before five and sets near nine; at midwinter it rises about half past eight and is gone by five. The sky, the colours and the lamps follow it, and so do the fish's dawn and dusk; the night passes quicker while it's dark, and you can sleep until dawn or the golden hour whenever they fall.
- **The warmth**: a few degrees warmer in summer, colder in winter (you feel it: see [How you feel](#how-you-feel-warmth-wet-clothes-thirst-and-hunger)); hardly any winter at Las Brasas.
- **The fish**: mackerel and the summer visitors (bass, mullet, garfish, gilthead, bonito, albacore, blue sharks) come in with the warm water and are gone or scarce in winter; bluefin come in late summer and autumn; squid crowd inshore in autumn and winter, cuttlefish in spring; pike and grayling come into their own as the year turns, while the lake's carp and tench go torpid in the cold. Las Brasas' own fish barely notice.
- **The rivers** run fuller in winter (and after rain), lower in summer.
- **The leaves**: fresh in spring, deep green in summer, gold and rust in autumn, dull brown in winter; the pines stay green, and the reeds turn to straw.
- **The people**: no cruising boats in winter (and fewer in autumn), no mackerel trips for Ian and no crabbing for Kit; the catch log records the season of every fish.

### Gales

The big storms off the Atlantic don't come out of nowhere: each **gale** is known a day ahead. The notice board gives the outlook for today and tomorrow ("Gale warning for today, from about 2:10 pm until 8:20 pm"), and they're rare: once in a couple of weeks in summer, two or three times a week in winter. A gale comes in over hours: the cloud thickens about seven hours before, the rain sets in, then heavy rain, then the storm itself (the wind at a gale, big breaking seas outside, thunder) for four to eight hours, and showers as it clears. You're warned **six hours** ahead (time to finish up and make for shelter) and again about **an hour and a half** before (if you're out past the bay, come in now); Bert's trip checklist flags one due within the next half day.

What to do with a gale day: the open sea goes dead and dangerous, but **sheltered water fishes**: the estuary, the coves, the bay, the lakes and rivers, where the fish feed hard in the blow and in the coloured water for hours after it. Or stay ashore: the inn, the market, the chandlery and the favours are all there, and the harbour's people (Dougie in the lee of the quay, Hettie on the sheltered side) are doing the same. Only Willow Bay's sea gets gales; Las Brasas' trade-wind weather hardly knows one. (Choosing a fixed sky in Settings turns them off.)

### Tides

The sea rises and falls twice a day, high water coming about fifty minutes later each day, and over a fortnight the tides grow from small **neaps** to big **springs** and back (a range of about a metre at springs in the game, a third of that at neaps). The water creeps up the beaches and the slipway and the boats rise with it.

- **Tidal streams** run out on the **ebb** and in on the **flood**, strongest at half tide and on springs, slack at high and low water: the race through Calf Sound, round Gannet Isle's points, over Long Shoal, through the Skerries and the cove channel, and the lower Wend, which the flood pushes back up its estuary. Your drifting rig and your boat feel them.
- **The fish of the sea feed best on a running tide** and go quiet at slack water; bass, mullet and the flatfish feed hardest on the flood. (The lakes' and rivers' fish don't care.)
- The **conditions panel** shows the tide (flooding, ebbing, high or low water now, and the next turn); the **notice board** gives the day's high and low waters and whether it's springs or neaps; the **catch log** records the tide each fish was caught on; Ruth's advice at the bar takes it into account; and Bert's trip checklist has it.

### Sea life to watch

Not everything in the sea is for catching. Keep an eye out:

- **Dolphins.** Bottlenose dolphins travel along the coast and pods of common dolphins (with a yellow and grey hourglass down each flank) roam the deep water; round Las Brasas, Atlantic spotted dolphins and bottlenose. They roll up to breathe as they go (you hear each one blow). Drive a boat along at a good clip near a pod and they come racing over to **ride your bow wave**: side by side just ahead of the stem, leaping clear now and then, whistling and clicking, until you slow down or they tire of it.
- **Grey seals.** Hauled out on Gannet Isle's west shore, on the biggest of the Skerries and on the Calf's skerry, lifting their heads to watch you. Lie quiet in a boat nearby (or dive) and a curious one comes up a few boat-lengths off to look you over, then slips under. Go along slowly near their rocks (rowing, dead slow, trolling) and now and then one **takes to following you**: up in your wake a few lengths astern, a snort, under, and up again behind you, for a few minutes, until you open the throttle or it loses interest. (Fishing boats are worth following, to a seal.)
- **Gannets.** Big white seabirds with black wingtips, wheeling round Gannet Isle by day. Where a shoal of small fish (sprats, mackerel, sardines) comes up near the surface, a flock gathers high above it and plunges in one after another, wings folded, with a white splash: a sign of fish below.
- **Whales.** A fin whale in the Blue Water and a minke whale along the shelf at home; a family of short-finned pilot whales off Las Brasas' south-west and a sperm whale far out beyond them. They travel deep for a few minutes, then surface to breathe a few times. Each breath is a **blow** you can see from far off (tall and narrow for the fin whale, bushy and slanting forward for the sperm whale) and hear close to. Then they arch down and are gone; the sperm whale lifts its flukes high as it dives.
- **Otters** on the quiet waters: the Coldwater's tideway below the Lower Force, the upper Wend above the quay, and the channels of the Saltings. Busiest at dawn and dusk. A long brown back and a thick tail along the bank, a dive with a flip of the tail, up again further on; now and then one comes up with a fish and hauls out on the bank to eat it. They're shy: walk up on one or come at it fast and it's gone for a good while. Keep still, sit, or drift down quietly, and you can watch.
- **Grey herons** standing in the shallows of Reedmere, on the Wend's sandbanks and round the Saltings: still as posts, stalking, a jab of the bill and sometimes a little fish. Come too close and one goes up with a harsh *fraank*, neck folded, slow wingbeats, and settles further off (or, from a small water, away over the hill until you've gone).
- **Cormorants** on the tops of Gull Rock's stacks, on the Skerries and on the Calf's skerry, often with their **wings hung out to dry**; others low in the water off the rocks, diving for half a minute and coming up a long way off. Come at their rock fast and they're off round in a loop.
- **Terns**, in spring and summer, over the bay and off Shell Strand: white, black-capped, buoyant; they hover over the shallows and **plunge in** after sand smelt, with a grating *kee-arr*.
- **Oystercatchers** along the shore by Shell Strand and on the Wend's sandbanks (when the tide's down: the flood pushes them off): black and white, long orange bills, probing the sand. Get close and the flock goes off low along the shore, **piping**, and lands again further on.
- **Cory's shearwaters**, round Las Brasas: big brown-and-white seabirds sweeping low over the waves on stiff wings, down on the boils where the barracuda and the tuna drive the bait up, and rafting on the sea off the Roque in the evening; after dark you hear them wailing as they come in to their burrows.
- **Loggerhead turtles** in Las Brasas' lee, drifting near the surface and up for a breath now and then; keep the boat slow near one, or dive with it.
- **On the crossing**: a big pod of common dolphins, a fin whale or a sperm whale, flying fish gliding away from the bow in the warm water, and the birds working over tuna (see [the crossing](#las-brasas-the-crossing-south)).
- **The sea glowing at night.** After dark, tiny plankton flash blue-green wherever the sea is stirred: your wake and bow wave, the oars, a splash, a fish darting just under the surface, a moving lure, a diver's kicks, and the dolphins' trails. Brighter some nights than others; never in the lakes and rivers.

The journal keeps what you've seen, under **Notes → Out on the water** (and **Memories → Sea life you've seen**): first seen when and where, and how many times; the day book lists them on each day's page. When something worth watching happens near you (an otter eating on the bank, a seal following, a heron catching a fish, a tern diving, a cormorant drying its wings) a quiet word says so, one at a time. Photographs know the wildlife in them, and Ruth at the Lobster Pot would very much like to hear if you ever see an otter.

### The fish finder

Press **F** for a small sonar screen. Drag its title bar to put it anywhere (it remembers the spot); double-click the title bar to put it back. The ⤢ button changes its size, and **?** explains the colours. In the boat it reads the water under the hull; on shore it works like a castable sonar, reading the water around your rig once it's in. The right edge, marked *now*, is the latest reading; older readings slide to the left. It shows the real bottom (a thick, bright line is hard rock; a soft one silt or sand), weeds as green strands, your bait or lure as a blue diamond, and fish as echoes: arches whose size and colour hint at how big they are, specks and clouds for small fish, and a little clutter. It never tells you what kind of fish it is, with one exception: a **bait ball** (a dense school of sprat, sardine or anchovy) shows as a cloud in its own sea-green, and the readout says *bait below* while you're over one; arches under it are what's hunting it. The readout also gives the water's temperature at the surface.

**The head unit.** Press **Unit** on the finder's title bar and it becomes a head unit, the kind of marine sonar a fishing boat carries: a moulded case with a recessed screen and a row of keys under it (an original design, not any maker's). Drag it by its top edge and resize it from its bottom-right corner; it remembers both, and every setting.

- **Pages** (the Page key, or the finder button: **D-pad ↓** / **F** steps through them and then puts it away): the **2D sonar**, echoes coloured by how strongly they come back, the bottom a hard band of colour fading down through what's under it, weed as strands off the bottom, fish as arches (a fish crossing the beam really does show as an arch: its echo comes back from the slant distance) or as **fish symbols** with the depth each was seen at, and a **flasher** strip at the right edge showing the ping going out now; the **down view**, the same echoes drawn the way a high-frequency imaging transducer shows them, almost a photograph of the bottom in one tint, with fish as bright little dashes; and the two **split** side by side.
- **Readouts** along the top: the depth and the kind of bottom, the water's temperature, and your speed in knots.
- **Range** (− and +): Auto, or a fixed scale from 3 to 40 m. **Zoom**: off, **2×** (half the range, following the bottom) or **bottom lock** (the bottom drawn flat along the foot of the screen, and the few metres above it, where the fish feeding on it are). **Gain** (0 to 10): turned up it picks out fainter and smaller echoes, with more clutter and speckle; turned down only the firm ones. **Colour**: classic blue, amber, daylight (for bright sun) or night red. **Fish**: symbols or arches.
- **Mark** drops a waypoint on your map where the sonar is reading, named with the depth and noting the bottom, the water's temperature and any fish under it.

### What's out there

The sea holds 52 real species of the coast from Royan and the Gironde estuary to Cádiz, each where it really lives (estuary channels and flats, rocks and reefs, sand and mud, shoals and open water), with the habits, baits and fights of its kind. The [field guide](#the-field-guide) has a page for each, with its model, where to find it and how to catch it; here they are in brief.

| Where | Species |
| --- | --- |
| The Wend estuary (brackish) | European seabass, spotted seabass, meagre, thicklip grey mullet, gilthead seabream, European flounder, common sole, Senegalese sole, wedge sole |
| Rocks, reefs and weed | ballan wrasse, black, white, two-banded and redbanded seabream, red porgy, common pandora, common dentex, rubberlip grunt, bastard grunt, pollack, European conger |
| Sand, mud and the seabed | European plaice, sand sole, turbot, tub gurnard, striped seabream |
| Shoals and open water | Atlantic mackerel, Atlantic horse mackerel, European sprat, European sardine, European anchovy, sand smelt, bogue, salema, garfish, bluefish, pompano, Atlantic bonito, little tunny, bullet tuna, Atlantic bluefin tuna |
| The Blue Water's big game | blue shark and shortfin mako (both released, on a wire trace), albacore (schools in summer; marmitako for supper) |
| Not fish | European squid, common cuttlefish, common octopus |
| To watch, not to catch | European eel (critically endangered: it ignores every bait) |

A few to start with: two-banded seabream round the weed and rocks of Willow Bay by day (a worm under a float); sand smelt schooling over the shallow sand (bread, kept shallow); sea bass in the estuary channel at dawn and dusk (a soft sandeel); sardines and horse mackerel shoaling under a light after dark (sabiki feathers); soles on the flats at night (ragworm on the bottom); an octopus in the rocks at dusk (a crab on the bottom, and a steady lift: it clings).

**Las Brasas**, the volcanic island across the open sea to the south (see [Las Brasas](#las-brasas-the-crossing-south)), has nineteen kinds of its own on top of the home coast's that live there too:

| At Las Brasas | Species |
| --- | --- |
| The shallow lava reef | parrotfish (the islands' favourite fish, “vieja”: a peeler crab on the bottom by day), ornate wrasse (bright, bold and a bait thief: let it go), blacktail comber (“cabrilla”: on every rock, it takes a prawn, a fish strip or a ragworm by the rocks; fried whole), grey triggerfish (a prawn; it picks, so wait for the tip to pull right round), Mediterranean moray (a fish strip on the bottom after dark; fried, a classic island dish) |
| El Veril, the drop-off | dusky grouper (in the caves of the wall and the arches off the Roque: catch and release, every one goes back, and never with the speargun), yellowmouth barracuda (shoals off the points at dawn and dusk: a soft sandeel on a wire trace), greater amberjack (the hardest-pulling fish in the game, on a metal jig with the offshore rod: let it go) |
| Shoals | Atlantic chub mackerel (the islands' mackerel: a small casting metal) |
| The blue water's big game | blue marlin, sailfish and longbill spearfish (all released), wahoo (“peto”, a fine supper), dorado (mahi-mahi), yellowfin tuna, smooth hammerhead (released); the blue shark, the mako and the albacore are here too |
| To watch, not to catch | Azores chromis (little dark damselfish in clouds over every reef, white tips to their tails), angelshark (critically endangered: a flat shark lying buried in the sand of El Sebadal, most about after dark), Atlantic trumpetfish (a long tube of a fish hanging head-down over the reef by day) |

The dusky grouper and the chub mackerel are in the Atlantic register; the other seventeen are fish of the warm eastern Atlantic islands outside it, listed in the field guide with their scientific names and where they live, like the freshwater fish.

Fresh water (Reedmere, Fernbeck and Longmere) keeps its own fish, outside the Atlantic catalogue: perch, pike, largemouth bass, mirror carp, tench, roach, rudd, brown trout and grayling, fished as before.

The five fish of Slow Tide's first version (glassminnow, sunperch, drizzlefin, duskfin and mossback) were invented. Real species have taken their places in the water (sand smelt, two-banded seabream, pollack, horse mackerel and conger), and the originals are retired: catches of them stay in your journal under their own names, and the field guide keeps their pages if you have them in your records.

Fish keep to their own waters: lake fish never turn up in the sea, nor sea fish in the lakes.

The places you fish from most have fish of their own, of the kinds that really haunt such places: at **Willowhaven**, grey mullet cruising the quay wall, gilthead and a conger in its stones, squid under the lamp after dark and sand smelt in the calm water inside; sand smelt and gilthead off the **lake landing's** pier; mullet and a bass or two at **Wend Quay**; perch, roach and a waiting pike under the **Longmere boathouse**. Every pool in **Fernbeck** holds trout or grayling (often both). None of them were taken from anywhere else: every other fish is where it always was.

### Offline play

Slow Tide is a Progressive Web App. The first time you open it online, it quietly stores the whole game on your device (scripts, styles, fonts, icons; the sounds and shaders are made by the code) and the title screen says **Ready to play offline**. After that it starts and plays with no connection at all: fishing, the journal and catch log, saving, and every place you've found. To add it to your home screen: on iPhone or iPad, Share → *Add to Home Screen*; on Android Chrome, ⋮ → *Install app*; on a computer, the install icon in the address bar.

**Which version you have** is on the title screen, under the buttons: *Version 67 · build 76580692 · 9 Nov 2026*. The version goes up with each change shipped (it's the number of the pull request that shipped it); the build is made from the game's own files (`src/version.js`, written by `tools/build-sw.mjs`, the same as the offline copy's version in `sw.js`), so two copies showing the same build are the same game.

**Getting the newest version: press Update** on the title screen, just above the version (or *Check for updates* in the journal, under Your progress). No need to refresh the page over and over:
- **If there's a new version**, it says *Downloading the new version (40%)…* as it comes down, then switches over by itself and the title shows the new number.
- **If you already have it**, it says *You have the newest version (Version 80)*.
- **If you're offline**, it says to connect first; your offline copy stays as it is.
- **If an update can't be stored** (the connection dropped, or the copy on this device is stuck), it fetches a clean copy of the game from the site instead. Your saved game, settings and photos are kept: they live in the browser's storage, not in the offline copy.

A new version found when you open the game downloads in the background too, and the title screen shows it coming down.

While you're offline, *Offline* appears next to the clock, and the journal explains that everything keeps working and saving on this device. If you've switched on cloud saves and signed in, syncing pauses; your progress waits in the browser and goes up when the connection is back, combined with anything newer from your other devices (catches are matched by id, so nothing is counted twice).

New versions arrive safely. When a new version is deployed, the game downloads it in the background and keeps running the old one. On the title screen it switches straight away once it's all down; during play it offers *Update to the new version* in the journal (it never reloads under you mid-game), and keeps your progress either way. Saves live in the browser's local storage, which updates never touch.

### Saving your progress

No account is needed. The game saves by itself in your browser: after every catch, release or kept fish, when you change rods or tackle, cook, eat, sleep, travel back to camp or the boat, every ten seconds while you play, and when you close or hide the page. Refresh or come back next week and you carry on where you were: the time of day and the weather, where you and each boat are, your gear, backpack, saved setups and creel, the camp fire and grill, your journal, catch log, the places you've found and the parts of the map you've explored, and your settings.

The journal's **Your progress** section shows when the game last saved and has three buttons:

- **Download a copy**: saves your whole game as a small `.json` file.
- **Load a saved copy…**: loads one of those files (it asks first, and keeps your current game as a backup).
- **New game…**: starts over after asking you to confirm, with an offer to download a copy first. Your sound and graphics settings stay. The old save is kept aside in the browser rather than deleted.

Your camp, map marks, the list of your photographs, adventures and keepsakes are saved too, and (from save version 6) where each piece of your outing gear is, the car, your plan, loadouts and last trip, and what's on the wall at home. Older saves load as they were: your camping gear stays where it was (at your camp, or rolled up in your backpack), the car waits on the drive at home, and you carry on wherever you were. A drive under way when the game closes finishes when you next load, with you and the car at the car park and nothing packed lost or doubled.

Saves are careful. Each one carries a version number, so a future update can convert old saves instead of breaking them. Every value is checked when loading, so a damaged save can't crash the game. If the save can't be read at all, it is kept aside (never written over) and the game loads the copy from the start of your previous session instead, and says so. A save made by a newer version is never written over. If you open the game in two tabs, the older tab stops saving so the two can't overwrite each other. In a private window, where the browser may refuse to store anything, the game tells you that progress won't be kept.

### The journal and catch log

Press **J**. The **Fish & places** pages hold a sketch and notes for every kind you've met, grouped by water (Willow Bay and the coves, the Wend estuary, the outer coast and open sea, fresh water, and *Not fish* for the squid), the hand-drawn map, the places you've found with what you've caught in each water, keepsakes and your progress. The **Catch log** pages record every catch:

- the kind and its length, and its weight (worked out from the length),
- the day and time, the water (salt, brackish or fresh), the nearest named spot, and the weather,
- how you caught it: the rod, the line, the bait, lure, fly or jig, and whether from a boat or the shore,
- how deep it took and how deep the water was, and whether flies were hatching,
- what happened to it: released, kept, cooked or eaten.

It shows your heaviest catch, totals and personal bests for each kind, and your recent catches with filters for kind, water, method and what happened to them (25 at a time, with *Show more*). The **Memories** pages hold your photographs, adventures and keepsakes, marks, camp and places (see [Photographs and memories](#photographs-and-memories)); **Days** is the day book and your record (below); **Notes** holds the grounds, the signs you've read and your field notes (see [Reading the water](#reading-the-water)).

### Sitting a while

Somewhere to sit: the log by the camp fire, your own camp's chair, the armchair by the stove at home, the bench under the lamp on Willowhaven quay (*Just sit and watch*), or back in the boat when she's at anchor or made fast (*Sit back a while*). Your angler sits down, the view settles over your shoulder on what's in front of you and drifts slowly round, and time goes by four times as fast. By the fire you hold your hands out to it and it warms you through (you keep it going without thinking about it); at the camp or at home the kettle goes on after a while. Now and then a quiet thought: weather on its way, the tide turning, the sun going down or coming up, the stars, an owl or a curlew, the day so far, something you've learned about the grounds, the season. Move, cast, or press the prompt's key to get up. The day book remembers it: *You sat a while by the camp fire as the evening came on.*

**The feel of the reel.** As well as the drag's ratchet (faster and brighter the faster line goes and the tighter it's set), the gears under load and the line singing near breaking, the drag knob now clicks notch by notch, higher and harder as you tighten it, so you can hear where it is; and on a cast the line hisses off the open spool, rising with the speed of the cast.

### The day book, your record and the harbour's challenges

Every day you go out is written up as a page of the journal (**Days**) when it ends, when you sleep or at midnight, and in the morning you're told so. It reads the way you'd write it yourself: the weather through the day; where you set out from, how (the boats you took) and where you went, and whether you came back, with the distance on the water, on foot, by car and swimming; what you caught, what you kept and what you let go; your first of a kind and your biggest yet; the dive sites you dived and how deep; the sea life you saw; the places you found; a while sat somewhere; the crossing to Las Brasas or home (setting out, landfall and how long at the helm, the fast passage, or turning back); and, if a gale blew, whether it caught you out on open water, you kept to sheltered water, or you sat it out ashore. Today's page fills in as you go. The book keeps the last sixty days.

Beside it, **Your record**: days written up, fish caught (kept and let go), kinds, places found, dives (the deepest and your longest breath-hold), dive sites and sea life seen; your best of each kind, the biggest for its kind first; and the fish to remember (near the top of the sizes their kind grows to).

**The harbour's challenges** are pinned on the board by the door of the Lobster Pot, in Ruth's hand: "Not a competition. Just something to go for." Ten from the bay, the coast slam (a bass, a pollack and a mackerel on one day), every water (sea, estuary, lake and river), twenty-five kinds, a specimen (the top tenth of its kind's sizes), fifty let go, after midnight, fished through a gale (from sheltered water), five dives (every home dive site), under the island (Las Brasas' five), thirty metres, Las Brasas, and a week on the water. Each one done puts a cloth badge on the cork board at home, between the record card and the stove. The harbour's people notice: they'll mention your new badge, or the dive site you were down at, for a day or so.

### The field guide

The journal's third tab, **Field guide** (also the **Field guide** button in the backpack's header), has a page for every species in the game: sea and estuary fish, the cephalopods, the freshwater fish (kept apart from the Atlantic catalogue), and the five fictional originals. Search by any name (English, French, Spanish, Portuguese or Latin) or filter to what you have or haven't caught. **Preview planned species** adds the researched species that aren't in the game yet, clearly marked as such.

Each page shows, before you've caught anything:

- **Identification**: its names, the same 3D model it has in the water (drag or use the arrow keys to turn it, scroll or + and − to zoom, ↺ to reset), its size in the game and in reality against a ruler, the features that tell it apart and what it's easily confused with.
- **Where to find it**: the waters, habitats and depths the simulation gives it, when it's most active and whether rain stirs it (read from the simulation's own functions, so the guide can't disagree with the game), a small map with the water that suits it shaded, **Show on the big map**, and a table of where it lives along the real coast from Royan to Cádiz, with how sure that is and what each region's word rests on. The game has no seasons yet, and the page says so rather than inventing any.
- **How to catch it**: the methods that work, the setup the guide recommends (rod, reel, line, rig or leader, bait or lure, each with its picture and every part accepted by the tackle rules, and a mouthful the fish can manage: a small-mouthed white seabream gets a ragworm, not a whole crab), how to present it, other setups that work, and the common mistakes for that species. **Rig it now** sets your rod up if you carry the parts; otherwise it says what's missing and where to get it, and **Open in the backpack** goes to the piece.
- **Your records**: how often you've seen it underwater (a fish in front of you within about nine metres counts), how many you've caught, your best, where, in what weather, and the setups that caught it.
- **Sources**: every page the register entry came from, and what is still uncertain.

In the backpack, each bait, lure, fly and jig lists the species it's **Good for** (✓ for ones you've caught); each opens that species' page. The catch card and the journal's pages show the same model as the guide (a pencil silhouette until you've caught one).

**Plan a fishing trip** (on any catchable species' page) puts it together:

1. **Where to go**: the stretches of water that suit it, nearest first, from the same habitat data the fish live by (waters, habitats, depths): areas of water, never where a fish is. Pick one; the map beside it rings it.
2. **What to take**: the guide's best setup and two alternatives, all accepted by the tackle rules.
3. **What you carry**: each part ticked if it's in your backpack, or *in the tackle box*; the line and rig come with the rod.
4. **Rig up**: *Rig it now* if you carry it; *Pack it and rig it* at a tackle box (camp or a motorboat's locker: if your backpack is full, the rod you aren't holding or tackle none of your rods uses goes back in the box, and you're told what); away from a box, what's missing and where to get it.
5. **Set off**: the water is marked on the map and with an on-screen marker until you get there.
6. **Afterwards**: the trip page shows how it's going (time out, the target caught, everything else), and *End the trip* keeps a record of it on the species' page. Nothing is guaranteed: fish move, feed and rest as they please.

**Why aren't they biting?** If a cast stays quiet for a while, the field guide offers one word on it (Settings → Controls & comfort → **Fishing hints**: gentle, more or off), and **Y** asks any time. The answer comes from the simulation itself, for your trip's fish (or the likeliest fish here): whether it eats what's on the hook and can manage the size, whether this is its kind of water and depth at this hour, whether your lure moves the way it likes, whether it's shy of your line, whether the current has carried your rig off, and how the fish that came to look behaved. It never says where a fish is.

### The map

Press **M** (or the map button). The map is drawn from the real ground and water depths, the whole chart from the start: every shore, path and place name, the places you haven't been yet in fainter ink, with a ring to go and find. (If you'd rather explore it, **Settings → Controls & comfort → The map: Explore it yourself** keeps it blank paper where you haven't been: land and water fill in around you as you travel, further out on the water than in the woods.) What you've explored and found is counted either way. It marks the places you've found (camps as tents, landings as little piers, landmarks with a cross), where each boat is, where you are, and where your best fish of each kind came from. Drag to move it, scroll or pinch to zoom, ◎ to centre on you. Aboard a boat (or the board, or the jet ski) you can keep the map open as you go: she carries on at her throttle notch (or under sail), the helm keys still steer and change the notch, and your mark moves across the map, filling it in as you travel. Beside it: your camps (with **Go there**), Willowhaven, your boat, your marks, and every place you've found. Your own camp shows as a tent in its own colour, marks as stars and flags, and (after *Where the gulls dive*) recent gull sightings as little birds. Opened from the field guide, it shades the water that suits a species and frames the nearest of it; this shows where to look, never where a fish is.

**On the chart** (the side panel): **Buoys & harbours** draws what a skipper steers by (`src/chartMarks.js`): the Wend Bar's red can and green cone, the yellow mark off the Shoal and the dive sites' buoys, the red can off Puffin Cove, the harbours and the anchorages; named once you zoom in. **Your catches** puts a dot where each fish of your log was landed (blue for the sea, green for fresh water).

**Routes** (`src/routes.js`): *Plan a route*, then click the chart for each mark in order (it starts where you are, up to sixteen marks). Each leg shows its course and distance, and the whole passage its time and fuel for the boat you'd take, from what she does at her cruise (the yard's trials: the RIB at 25 knots, the console at 22, the skiff at 10…), with a warning if it's more than her tank or cutting it fine. Save it (up to eight), see it on the chart, or *Follow* it: with a chartplotter fitted, the helm shows the next mark, its course and how far, and as you round each mark (within 35 m) it moves on to the next and tells you the new course; the last one, and the route's done.

### Settings

Press **O** (or the gear button, or *Settings* on the title screen). Everything is kept with your game and changes take effect straight away. Opening a menu never casts, reels or steers.

- **Sound**: master volume and mute, music, surroundings, **wind**, **waves**, rain, effects, the **reel and line** (the drag's clicks, the reel's whirr and the singing line; turn it down for a quieter fight), and the bite-alert bell (with a button to hear it). Mute silences everything, wind and waves included. If the sound cuts out, comes and goes or stutters (often headphones, Bluetooth or a voice call taking the speakers), **Restart sound** connects it to your speakers again, as minimising the window and bringing it back does; the game does the same by itself whenever your speakers or headphones change. The sound runs a few hundredths of a second behind on purpose: a roomy buffer, so a busy computer (the game drawing, a session going) doesn't break it up. **Sound quality: Light** swaps the long, rich echo for a simple one, a fraction of the work, for computers where the sound crackles or hisses; and on every setting the sound's layers that are silent just then (the stream, the fire, the pub's murmur, the race in Calf Sound and the rest) are switched off until they're wanted, which halves the sound's work at sea. The sound always runs at 48,000 samples a second, whatever the sound card is set to, and a limiter keeps the loudest moments from crackling.
- **Graphics**: a preset (**Auto**, **Cinematic**, **Ultra**, **High**, **Medium**, **Low**) and, under it, resolution, shadows, reflections and water detail, plants and particles, underwater effects, how many distant fish are drawn, and a frame-rate cap. Auto starts at High on computers and Medium on phones and tablets, then steps down by itself if the game runs slowly; it never picks Ultra or Cinematic for you. **Restore recommended settings** puts everything back to Auto in one tap. Lower settings draw less but change nothing about the fishing: every fish is still simulated, can still bite and still shows on the fish finder.
  - **Ultra** is the best that still plays smoothly on a computer with a good graphics card: drawn a little sharper than the screen (even an ordinary monitor), finer shadows reaching further round you, a full-resolution mirror on the water and a finer ripple field, about a quarter more grass and underwater weed, clearer air and a longer view, and a light finishing pass: a soft glow round the brightest light (the sun and its glints, lamps, lit windows) and the colours of the hour graded a little.
  - **Cinematic** is the richest look, for powerful computers and photographs: everything Ultra has, sharper again on ordinary screens, half as much grass and weed again as High, the clearest air and the longest view, and a fuller finishing pass: a stronger glow, warm light streaming from a low sun at sunrise and in the golden hour, a touch more colour and a gentle darkening at the corners. It can run at a lower frame rate, especially on laptops and phones, and Settings says so when you pick it.
  - **Ocean** (Auto, Full or Simple) sets how much of the sea is worked out. **Full** works the short waves (from about eight metres down to a few centimetres) out on the graphics card every frame, so the water close by has its own texture, glitter and lacy foam, and draws the boats' wakes out to half a kilometre. **Simple** leaves the short waves to the shading and draws wakes nearer in: for phones, tablets and slower computers. **Auto** picks Full on a computer at Medium or above whose browser can do it (WebGL 2 with float render targets), and Simple on a touch screen or at Low; the line under the setting says which it chose. The long waves, the ones a boat rides, are the same in both.
  - The world is still a stylised, low-poly one at every level: the top levels make it cleaner, deeper and warmer, not photographic. Changing the level never moves the camera, interrupts a fish you're playing or touches your save (beyond remembering the choice). The finishing pass needs WebGL 2; where a browser hasn't got it, Ultra and Cinematic simply go without it.
  - **Screen** (Full or Clean): **Clean** shows just the icons and keys along the top (the label comes back on hover), brings up the weather panel only while you're fishing or sailing, and tucks the rod pill away while you're under way: more sea, less screen. Full is as it always was.
- **Weather & sea**: Clear, Partly cloudy, Overcast, Light rain, Heavy rain, Storm, or Automatic (it changes by itself through the day). The sky, light, fog, water, rain, sound and the fish all follow it, changing smoothly. Separate sliders for how much rain is drawn and how loud it is. Then the **sea** (with the weather, Calm, Moderate or Lively), the **wind** (automatic, or set it yourself: direction and strength), the **currents** (Natural; Gentle, at half strength; or Still, where your boat and rig stay put and only the fish know the water runs), and whether the conditions panel shows.
- **Controls & comfort**: camera sensitivity, invert look, reduced motion, a **boat camera** that moves with the boat or keeps a steady horizon (no pitch or roll, and the quick bob of the chop smoothed away; you still drift gently up and down with the swell, so the view never looks detached from the water), assisted or manual casting, **fishing hints**, difficulty, **fish behaviour**, **character needs** (Gentle, Shown only or Off: see [How you feel](#how-you-feel-warmth-wet-clothes-thirst-and-hunger)), and every key, with a button to change each one (press the new key; one already in use swaps over).
- **You** (`src/looks.js`): how you look, on foot and at the helm of every boat, and to friends playing with you: your skin (a broad range of tones), your raincoat, the jumper under it and the shirt under that, your trousers and boots, a hat (the sun hat, a cap, a beanie, a bucket hat, a flat cap, or none, your hair loose or up) and its colour, your hair, a beard and glasses. Your clothes keep you warm and dry as ever whatever their colour, and the woolly hat still comes out when it's cold. Friends see you as you made yourself (sent with your name); a friend who hasn't chosen is drawn from their name, as before.
- **Play together**: your name, starting or joining a session with friends (see [Playing together](#playing-together-optional)), and a **friend code**: a code from a friend gives you boats at Willowhaven Marine Works, money in your purse, or both. There's one for every boat and a little money and one for the boats alone, each once for each game, and one for money alone that works as often as you type it (handy for testers; see `src/giftcodes.js`, and `node tools/gift-code.mjs YOUR-CODE [money] [name]`, with `--boats-only` or `--money-only` and `--reusable`, to make one: the game keeps only each code's fingerprint, never the code).

Graphics settings never change the simulation: the waves the boats ride, the wind on a cast, the currents and every fish are the same on Low as on Cinematic.

At every level the sky now glows warm round a low sun and turns a cooler dusky blue and rose on the far side, with a proper sunrise and a blue hour after dusk; overcast light is softer and more even; and after dark the windows of the haven and the cabin boat glow with warm lamplight.

A note on what this is: Slow Tide is a stylised, low-poly game drawn with three.js and WebGL in the browser. The water uses real planar reflections, a ripple simulation and procedural detail. The lighting, haze and mist are painted rather than physically simulated, and nothing here is photorealistic. Far-off places are drawn only when you're near them, and fish far from you are simulated more coarsely, but never stop.

### Playing together (optional)

Friends can share the water: each of you plays your own game, in one shared world, and you see each other in it.

- **Your name.** The first time you press *Begin*, the game asks what to call you (it suggests one, so Enter is enough). Friends see it over you. Change it whenever you like in **Settings → Play together**; it's kept with your settings, so a new game keeps it.
- **Start a session.** In **Settings → Play together**, choose *Start a session*. You get a **four-digit code**: tell it to a friend.
- **Join one.** Your friend opens **Settings → Play together**, types the code under *Join a session* and presses *Join*. Up to eight can play together.
- **In a session** you see each other where you really are: on foot (each of you with a look of your own, made from your name) or in whichever boat you're in, at the helm, with the sail up or a rod out. Your name floats over you, and when a friend is out of sight a tag at the edge of the screen points the way to them, with how far. You hear about each other's catches (what, and how heavy, and whether it's a first or a best), and who joins and leaves.
- **Finding each other.** Friends are on the map (M) too, in their own colour: an arrow for a boat with which one it is, a dot ashore. While it's open it keeps up with them.
- **Quick calls.** In a session a **Call** button appears at the top (or press **4**): *Over here!*, *Fish on!*, *Come and look at this*, *Good spot here*, *Wait for me*, *Heading in*. Friends hear it with how far and which way you are, and see it beside your name for a few seconds.
- **What they're fishing with.** A friend fishing holds the rod they're really using (on foot, or in their boat), and their line runs from its tip out to where it meets the water: their float riding the swell if it's a float rod, the line sagging and lying on the water while it's slack, and following their lure as it comes in. Under their name you see their setup (*spinning rod, spinner*), and Settings → Play together says it too (*fishing from the shore (float rod, sweetcorn)*).
- **A fish on.** When a friend hooks something you hear about it (once in a while, not for every fish), and see their rod up and bent, working the fish, the line tight and straight out to the fish as it runs, harder bent the more load there is on it, until it's landed or gone. You see their fish fight at the end of the line as they do: the water boiling where it runs off, a crash of spray as it leaps, spray as it shakes its head at the top, a wake as it charges or streaks away, nothing at the top while it's down deep; and their tag says what it's doing (*fish on! it's sounding*). When they land it, their tag says what it was for a few seconds (*european seabass, 2.4 kg!*), and on foot they hold it up for you to see.
- **Each other's boats.** Your friends see all your boats in the water where you've left them (at most three, so it's never a crowd), not only the one you're in; they lie alongside riding the swell, their lines out, and they're solid: your boat bumps off a friend's as off any other, and the harbour's skippers steer round them.
- **A guest in a friend's harbour.** Joining someone's session, you're a visitor at their Willowhaven: the finger with the plate is theirs (the plate shows their name while you're there), and your Willowhaven boats lying in the harbour move to the **visitors' pontoon**, the end row, each guest a finger or a side of their own, the same on every screen. Boats you put in the water or bring round while you're a guest go there too. When you leave the session, they go back to your own finger.
- **The same berth.** Boats from the same jetty (the camp's landing, say) start in the same spot, so while a friend's boat lies right on top of one of yours, yours steps out of sight; it's back as soon as they move off (and boarding it works as ever).
- **One sea for everyone** (`src/sharedworld.js`). In a session you share the same sea, not just the same sky: the same whales surfacing and blowing, the same dolphin pods (riding whichever boat drew them in: on your screen they're on your friend's bow, and on theirs on yours), the same seals in the water (one that's taken to following your friend's boat follows it on your screen too), the same turtles, the same gannets and shearwaters coming down on the same shoal, and the same shoals, bait balls and hunters crashing through them. Spot birds working, call your friend over, and they'll find the same frenzy. The host's game keeps the sea going round everyone (a friend in another bay, or at Las Brasas, has their own water alive) and sends what it all is and is doing twice a second; everyone else's sea follows that. Join late, drop out and come back, or have the host reload, and you see what's there now, never two of anything. Meeting a whale on the crossing as a guest asks the host to bring it up, so everyone sees it.
- **The same fish.** The fish that take a lure are the same fish for everyone too. Your float or lure is in the host's water as well as yours, and the host's fish decide whose rig to go for, one fish to a rig and one rig to a fish, however many of you cast at the same shoal. A fish coming to your rig comes to it on your screen at once, and when it takes, it's yours: strike, and the host checks it really was taking your rig just then (if it had already let go, the hook comes back empty, and the game tells you so). While you play it, nobody else can hook it, and your friends see that very fish fighting at the end of your line, running, leaping, deep while it dives, and as itself: your game sends what it is and how big (and when it's landed), so on their screen the take erupts, the fish itself clears the water in a leap as high as its kind and size go, streaming water, head shaking, and crashes back in by its size (a marlin tail-walking and greyhounding on), a run along the top leaves its V, your line throws spray where it cuts the water, it thrashes at the end, and comes out with a splash as you land it, as you see it yourself (`src/fightshow.js`). They hear the splashes if they're near enough (fading out by sixty metres), and see your line out to the fish even on a long run, up to 700 metres. Land it and keep it and it's gone from everyone's water; let it go and it swims off for everyone; and in its place, a while later, a newcomer turns up, the same size and the same fish on every screen. Close the game mid-fight and the fish is off for the others.
- **Helping each other.** When a friend's fish is beaten and lying alongside waiting for the net, and you're close enough (on foot by them, or your boat alongside), the prompt offers to **net it for them**: it goes into the net on their screen, it's their catch (their log, their creel, their record), and you hear what it was. And when you find a shoal, the quick calls (the call button, or its key) offer **Mark this shoal**: a label goes over it on your friends' screens and follows that very shoal as it moves, for ten minutes or until it's been fished out.
- **Diving together.** Dive near a friend who's diving and you see them in the water as they are: their diver swimming where they swim, as deep as they are, head down as they go down and fins kicking harder when they kick, in their own kit (the yellow tank on scuba, or the snorkel and speargun spearfishing), the stripe on their suit their own colour, with their name over them under the water. Water is murky, so you see a friend's diver within about 160 m.
- **Crewing.** Walk up to a friend's boat lying stopped by the shore or a jetty, or bring yours alongside and stop (anchored, tied up or still), and the prompt offers to **step aboard**. You stand in their boat and go where it goes, on every screen; walk about her, fish from her, watch your fish finder, and net their fish for them. The prompt while you're aboard offers **the helm**: if they hand it over, your keys steer their boat and work her throttle (or oars) while they fish, or to follow a big fish on a run; the moment they touch the controls at the wheel it's theirs again. Step back into your own boat alongside, or ashore at a jetty or the beach, the same way. If their boat goes (they leave, or step ashore), you're put back in your own boat, or where you stepped aboard; and a save while you're crewing keeps you there too, never standing on the sea. Several friends can crew one boat: each steps aboard to a place of their own in her (aft of the helm on either side, beside it, then forward), clear of the skipper and of each other. In a motorboat **her locker is the crew's too**: change your rod and tackle aboard as the skipper does. The skipper is told as each friend steps aboard and off.
- **Everyone's paid.** Sell fish while you're playing together (at the market, at Kaigan or to a kitchen's order) and everyone in the session is paid the same as you, into their purse with a word saying who sold what and where: a good day out pays the whole crew. Don't want to? **Settings → Play together → Fish you sell: Keep it to myself**. It's the seller's choice, and only so much can come from any one friend in a while.
- **What it costs.** The Play together tab shows how many messages the session has sent and received, about how much data, and how often the connection had to be got back: Supabase counts messages against its monthly allowance. Under it, **Network details** breaks that down by kind (where everyone is, the shared sea, time and weather) with each one's rate, and how fresh and how big the shared sea is. Nothing is sent anywhere else. The shared sea is the biggest part by size: a snapshot of 1–5 KB twice a second with friends close together, up to about 10 KB (and then once a second) with eight players far apart. By count, everyone's movements are: Supabase counts each message once when it's sent and once for every player it reaches, against a per-second limit on the project's plan (100 a second on the Free plan, 500 on Pro), and Network details shows the whole session's rate against those. Measured with everyone on the move and half of them fishing (`node --import ./tests/setup.mjs tools/netbudget.mjs 8`): about 47 a second for three players, 75 for four, 113 for five and 256 for eight. So up to four play comfortably on the Free plan, and eight need Pro.
- **Trying it on one machine.** `node tools/local-realtime.mjs` runs a stand-in for Supabase Realtime locally, so two browser windows can play together with no Supabase project (see the file for how to point the game at it).
- **One world.** Whoever started the session (the host) sets the world for everyone: the time of day, the weather, the wind and the sea, so you share the same dusk, the same rain and the same tide, and the fish feed by the same clock. When you join, the sky sweeps to the host's in a few seconds; when you leave, your own time and weather sweep back. While you're following the host's world, the weather settings say so, and your own choices there come back when you leave.
- **Time is the host's to pass.** Only the host can rest (hold T), sleep the night or wait a while; when they do, everyone sees the hours go by. Anyone else trying is told so. Travelling still takes you where you're going.
- **Everything else stays your own**: your own day and its day book, favours, the fish on ice, your gear, money (but for friends' fish sold, above) and progress all keep your own time underneath, and none of the host's world is saved into your game. A fish you catch is yours: it goes in your log, your creel and your records, and nobody else's.
- **Reloads.** Reloading the page (or pressing *Update*) in the middle of a session puts you back into it. If the host reloads, everyone else waits for them: the world comes back as soon as they do.
- **Leaving.** *Leave the session* and you're out of it. When the host chooses *End the session* it ends for everyone; if the host closes the game, or loses the connection and doesn't come back within a minute, the session ends and everyone's own world comes back. If your own connection drops, the game tries to get it back for a minute or so before giving up.
- **Sharing what you find.** With *Share what you find* on (it is, unless you choose *Keep to myself* in the Play together tab), your friends hear about what you find and get it on their map as a mark in sea green with your name on it: a place you've found for the first time, a dive site you've dived, something found on a dive (the Kittiwake's bell, the arch), and a **tip** for each kind of fish you catch for the first time (where, on what, when, and the tide). A place they know already is just news. You can send any of your own marks too: open the map, edit the mark and choose *Send to friends*. Marks from friends stay on your map (and in Memories) until you delete them, like your own. A few at a time, at most six in half a minute.
- **When something's wrong** the Play together tab says what, and what to do: the online service paused (it can be restored from the Supabase dashboard), its allowance used up, or a friend on a newer version of the game (reload to update).

Playing together needs the same backend as [cloud saves](#cloud-saves-optional): with none set up, the tab says so and nothing else changes. Nobody needs to sign in to play together.

### How the boats move

The boats aren't animated: each is a rigid body floating in the water, worked out many times a frame (`src/hydro.js`), the way a driving simulator works out a car.

- **Her hull** is a closed mesh of a few hundred panels. Each panel under the water feels the water's pressure at its depth (so she floats as deep as her real weight sinks her: a skiff with its motor and you about 290 kg, the sailing dinghy 260 kg, the sport-fishing yacht 9 tonnes) and the water pushing back as it moves through it, lifting a fast boat's bow onto the plane, damping her heave and roll, and slamming as she drops off a crest. Ahead she meets her own measured resistance. Sideways she grips about as real hulls do: checked against naval architects' measurements of how hulls resist sliding and turning (Clarke's formulas), within a factor of about two. The water's inertia swings a hull going a little sideways further round (the Munk moment), as it does on real boats.
- **Keels, centreboards, rudders, skegs, fins and outboard legs** are little wings: lift across the flow and drag along it, stalling past their best angle. The rudder only bites with way on; the dinghy's centreboard stops her sliding to leeward; an outboard's leg turns with the tiller; the yacht's rudders sit in her propellers' wash, so they steer even at idle.
- **Propellers, the jet and the oars** push where they are, the way they point: an outboard swings its thrust with the tiller, the jet ski's nozzle turns only while it's pushing, the yacht's twin screws go one ahead and one astern to walk her round where she is, oars pull at their oarlocks, a paddle on alternate sides. A prop or jet lifted clear of the water stops biting.
- **The sails** (`src/sailing.js`) push high up on the mast, so she heels; you hike out on the windward side, and in the gusts you spill a little wind. Released mid-tack, you finish the tack; stopped in irons you back the sail to fall off. A standing rider (jet ski, windsurf board) keeps her level with their weight, fore and aft as well as side to side.
- **Ropes and contacts**: the anchor rope is elastic nylon, the mooring lines draw her in and hold her off the fenders, the tow line pulls (never pushes), and every rope gives way before it would pull a boat apart. Her keel and chines touch the seabed and the beach (she grinds to a halt with a scrape), and she bumps off piers, quays and other boats.
- **The helm** goes over at the pace a hand turns a tiller, a wheel or the bars, not in an instant, and the faster she goes the less of it you put on, as anyone driving a fast boat does: a tap of the key at speed is a gentle change of course, held over she sweeps round in a curve rather than spinning out, and let go she settles on her new heading (hands off, you hold the course she settles on). On the jet ski you lean into the turns, and its pump housing and sponsons keep it running straight.
- **The waves under her** are the same waves you see, sampled under her own length and beam, and other boats' wakes run under her too.

It costs under a millisecond a frame per boat.

### Wind, waves and currents

One shared set of conditions (`src/world/conditions.js`) moves the water you see, the boats on it, your float and rig, the cast, the trees and the sound:

- **Wind** has a direction, a sustained strength that changes slowly with the weather (or as you set it), and **gusts**: patches of stronger air drifting downwind, visible as darker ruffles on the water. Like a real Atlantic coast it's seldom still: a breeze most days (about 15 to 30 km/h), and on a fair day a **sea breeze** sets in towards midday, blowing in from the sea and freshening through the afternoon (often 30 to 45 km/h), then dies away in the evening, with lighter air at night. At **Las Brasas** the **trade wind** blows steadily from the north-east, 25 to 45 km/h and fresher in the afternoon, as it does in the Canaries. Land breaks it: bays, coves and the lee of a shore get less. It pushes a cast in flight (light tackle and long casts most), drifts an unanchored boat (the high-sided bass boat most) and a float's tip a little, bends the trees and grass, and you hear it rise in the gusts.
- **Waves** are long **swell** from the open Atlantic (west-south-west), which builds and fades over minutes (some days a bigger one is running) and hardly reaches into the bays, the coves or the estuary, plus a **wind sea** raised by the local wind, which builds in under a minute and is smaller where the wind has little room. The wind sea (`src/world/ocean.js`) is thousands of waves drawn at random from the spectrum a real one has for that wind and fetch (JONSWAP, spread round the wind's direction), each running at its own speed, summed by a fast Fourier transform the way film and game oceans are (after Tessendorf): in light air it's all short, steep little waves; as the wind freshens it peaks in longer ones, about half a metre crest to trough in a moderate breeze and a metre or more in a strong one out in the open, with sharp crests and long troughs. The waves from about six metres up are worked out twenty times a second over a 140 m patch, laid twice over the sea (the second turned and shifted) so it never visibly repeats; the boats float on exactly these, sampled the same way the water is drawn. With **Ocean: Full** (Settings → Graphics) the short waves, from about eight metres down to a few centimetres, are worked out on the graphics card every frame from the same spectrum, carried on smoothly where the long ones leave off: only for the eye, the texture and glitter of the water close by. Changing the wind turns and builds the sea gradually; nothing jumps. Running into shallow water the swell grows and steepens over the last few metres, **breaks** in white on the exposed beaches and is spent before the shallows, so boats sit still on a beach; each wave that's broken **runs up the sand** in a thin sheet of foam and slides back. From a moderate breeze up, the crests the waves pinch tightest break into **whitecaps**, on as much of the sea as measured whitecap cover for that wind (Monahan's), a few at first and more the fresher it blows: each a white core with lace trailing downwind that fades over a few seconds. With the sun beyond them, light glows green through the thin tops of the crests. A rough sea breaks up reflections: clouds and the shore mirror only on calm water.
- **Wakes** (`src/world/wakes.js`): every boat under way leaves a Kelvin wake, the pattern any vessel makes in deep water: inside a wedge 19.5° either side of her track, the feathered divergent waves angling out, the transverse waves across her stern, and the biggest crests along the wedge's edge. It follows the track she actually took and goes on spreading as it ages; a fast boat's looks narrower, as real ones do. How big it is follows her weight, her length and how fine her hull is, and her speed: a kayak's is a few centimetres, a yacht pushing hard at about her hull speed makes half a metre or more, smaller again once she's planing. The townsfolk's boats make wakes too, and every wake lifts and rocks any boat it reaches. Close round a hull under way the water heaps up: a **bow wave** peeling off each shoulder, a trough along her sides and, at displacement speeds, a **stern wave** rising behind her transom, all of which a boat alongside feels; **spray** flies from wherever a hull is actually cutting into the water (`src/spray.js`): off the shoulders of a displacement boat, in sheets from a planing hull's chines (or from under the RIB's tubes) well aft of her bow, off the lee side of a heeled sailing boat, and in a burst from her whole bottom when she drops off a crest, more the harder she drives into it; a fast boat leaves white water at the foot of her bow wave, and a working propeller churns a lacy **wash** of foam astern that spreads and fades. Beyond it, every boat pushing the water about leaves a long **foam trail** on the sea (`src/world/foamTrails.js`), lying along the way she actually went (round a turn it curves, and you can cross your own track), widening and breaking into lace as it ages and fading over a minute or more, so a fast boat's track stays on the water hundreds of metres back; a planing hull or a hard-working propeller leaves a bright lane, a boat gliding along at a walking pace next to none. A wake that reaches a **quay wall or a pier** comes back off it, weaker, and runs back out across the water (never through the wall); and with **Ocean: Full** wakes are drawn out to half a kilometre, so you can watch a boat's wake crossing the bay.
- **Currents**: a gentle drift along the open coast; the Wend running out to sea, fastest in the narrow river, easing over the estuary; and faster water in the channel into Driftwood Cove, round Gannet Isle's points, through the Skerries and over Long Shoal. Sheltered water is slack, and every zone fades smoothly into the next. Water at the seabed runs at about 60% of the surface's. A float goes with the water it sits in; a lure is carried while you work it (its speed through the water is what the fish judge); a jig drifts as it sinks; a bottom rig is carried as it falls, then its lead holds unless the current at the bottom is stronger than its weight can stand (a 5 oz flapper rig holds where a light feeder would roll); fish holding station face into it. The current zones are built for a future tide: today they all run on the ebb.

- **Storms**: now and then heavy rain turns into a storm (or choose **Storm** in Settings → Weather & sea). The wind rises to a gale, with harder gusts and a howl in it; over a minute or two the big seas roll in from the Atlantic, far longer and higher than the everyday swell (four to seven metres crest to trough on the open coast), standing up in steep peaks with long troughs between, their crests breaking white and the foam blown down the wind in streaks; spray flies off the breaking crests and off your bow as she plunges into them. The sky goes low and dark slate, the rain drives slantwise, and lightning strikes out over the sea and the hills (flashing the whole sky, often twice) with the thunder rolling in after it, later the further off it struck (a kilometre in three seconds). Every boat is thrown about far more, but none is ever tipped over. The coast still shelters: the bay, the coves and the estuary stay much calmer, and the big seas have spent themselves before the shallows, so a storm is a time to run for home (the game says so if you're out in the rowing boat, the kayak or under sail). A storm takes longer to die away than to come. With **Reduce motion** on (Controls & comfort), the lightning never flashes the screen; the thunder still rolls.

The **conditions panel** (top left, **N**) shows the wind (speed in km/h, where it blows from, arrows as you see them, *gusting* or *sheltered*), the current at your rig or under you (m/s) and the sea state in words (glassy, rippled, smooth, slight, moderate) with the wave height. Calm conditions are a complete way to play: every fish can be found in calm water. If you'd like it quieter, Settings → Weather & sea → **Sea: Calm** keeps light airs and a gentle swell whatever the weather.

**Wind lines** (the white streaks in the air while you sail, or always, or never: Settings → Weather & sea) show the wind's direction and strength; in a strong wind they drift past more slowly than the wind itself, with short tails, so they never streak across the view.

### Cloud saves (optional)

Cloud saves let a player sign in with their email and carry their game between devices. They are **off** until you connect a backend; until then nothing about accounts appears in the game. They use [Supabase](https://supabase.com) (it has a free tier), talking to it directly from the browser with the public "anon" key. Row level security in the database makes sure each player can only ever read or change their own save.

To switch them on:

1. Create a project at [supabase.com](https://supabase.com) (*New project*; any name, region and database password).
2. In the project, open **SQL Editor → New query**, paste in the contents of [`supabase/schema.sql`](supabase/schema.sql) and press **Run**. This creates the `slow_tide_saves` table and its security rules.
3. Open **Authentication → URL Configuration**. Set **Site URL** to `https://fishing-game-weld.vercel.app`. Under **Redirect URLs** add `https://fishing-game-weld.vercel.app/**` and, for branch previews, `https://fishing-game-*.vercel.app/**`.
4. Email sign-in is on by default (**Authentication → Sign In / Providers → Email**). Supabase's built-in email sender only allows a few emails an hour and is meant for testing. For real players, add your own email provider under **Authentication → Emails → SMTP Settings**.
5. Open **Project Settings → API Keys** (or **Data API**) and copy the **Project URL** and the **anon / publishable** key. Never use the `service_role` / secret key: the game and the config function both refuse one.
6. In **Vercel → your project → Settings → Environment Variables**, add `SUPABASE_URL` (the Project URL) and `SUPABASE_ANON_KEY` (the anon key) for *Production* and *Preview*. Then redeploy (**Deployments → ⋯ → Redeploy**).
7. Open the game, press **J**, and look under **Your progress**: a **Cloud save (optional)** box with *Email me a sign-in link* should appear. If it doesn't, open `/api/cloud-config` on your site: it shows `{"enabled": false}` until both variables are set.

How it behaves:

- **Guests keep playing.** Signing in is never required.
- **Signing in keeps what you've done.** The first time, your progress in that browser is uploaded. On another device it is **combined** with your cloud save rather than replaced: every logged catch is kept (matched by its id), each fish keeps the higher count and the bigger best, and places and keepsakes from both are kept. Where you are, the time of day, your gear, creel and camp come from whichever was saved most recently.
- **Two devices at once** don't overwrite each other: each save carries a revision number, and if another device saved in between, the game takes in its catches and tries again.
- **New game** or **Load a saved copy** while signed in also replaces the cloud save, and the confirmation says so.
- **Signing out** leaves your progress in the browser. Deleting a user in Supabase (**Authentication → Users**) deletes their save too.

Not on Vercel? Put the URL and anon key in `src/cloud-config.js` instead. The anon key is designed to be public; never put any other key there.

**Playing together** uses the same project, and needs nothing more: no table, no sign-in. It goes through [Supabase Realtime](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/realtime): each session is a public broadcast channel named after its code (`slow-tide-4821`), with Realtime's presence saying who is in it. Nothing is written to the database. Two things to know:

- Realtime must allow public channels, which it does unless you've changed it (in the project's Realtime settings, leave *Allow public access* on). If you turn it off, playing together stops working; cloud saves don't mind.
- While someone moves, the game sends about four small messages a second (one every few seconds while they keep still), and each one is delivered to everyone else in the session. Supabase counts Realtime messages against your plan's monthly allowance, so keep an eye on **Usage** if lots of people play together.

Anyone who knows (or guesses) a session's four-digit code can join it while it's open, so it's for playing with friends, not for keeping anyone out.

## The Atlantic species register

Slow Tide's sea is meant to grow into the coast from Royan and the Gironde estuary, along the French Bay of Biscay, the Cantabrian coast, Galicia and Portugal, to the Algarve and the Gulf of Cádiz. The register (`src/register.js`, with the researched entries in `src/registerData.js`) is the checklist of fish and cephalopods for it, and the one source of truth for names and real-world facts across the field guide, the journal, the catch card and the water.

It is a researched catalogue, **not a complete inventory** of everything recorded on this coast: 62 species so far (55 fish, 3 cephalopods, 2 sharks and 2 rays), the ones an angler, snorkeller or fish market of this coast would meet, not the deep-water fish or rare visitors. Each entry has:

- a stable id, the scientific name with its author, synonyms and family;
- English, French, Spanish and Portuguese names where a source gives them (the shanny has no Spanish name yet, and none is made up);
- habitat, usual and recorded depths, typical and exceptional size (with the source of each), diet, behaviour and seasonal movements;
- for each of the six regions: resident, seasonal, occasional, absent or unknown, with a confidence (high, medium or low) and what it rests on. A range statement alone ("Morocco to Norway") gives at most medium confidence; a regional checklist, survey or official document is needed for high;
- identification features, look-alikes, angling methods, conservation and regulations;
- its sources (578 links in all), and what is uncertain or conflicting.

The research came from FishBase, SeaLifeBase, DORIS, WoRMS and the Catalog of Fishes, regional checklists and papers (the Portuguese marine checklist, the Galician and Cantabrian lists and IEO guides, the Andalusian catalogue, Ifremer and AZTI surveys, ICES and ICCAT documents) and French, Spanish and Portuguese official rules. It lives in `research/register/*.json`; `node tools/build-register.mjs` compiles it into `src/registerData.js` (a test checks the two match). Stock assessments and landings were used only as evidence of presence, never as counts of species. Of the 372 regional statements, 153 are high confidence, 156 medium and 63 low.

Four things are kept apart: the **research catalogue** (all 64), the **playable roster** (the 54 you can catch), **observation-only wildlife** (in the water to be watched, never caught) and **planned or uncertain records**. Each card and page says which:

- **In the game · catchable now**: 58 species from the register, and the minnow (fresh water). Every one has an anatomical model, lives in its habitats, has a setup the field guide recommends that the tackle rules accept, is tested biting on it in the headless ecosystem and landed with it (careful play, Standard difficulty), and is logged in the journal.
  - Bass and the estuary: European seabass, spotted seabass, meagre, thicklip grey mullet.
  - Seabreams and grunts: gilthead, black, white, two-banded, striped and redbanded seabream, red porgy, common pandora, common dentex, rubberlip grunt, bastard grunt.
  - Rock and open-water predators: pollack, European conger, bluefish, ballan wrasse.
  - Flatfish and the seabed: common, Senegalese, sand and wedge sole, turbot, European flounder, European plaice, tub gurnard.
  - Shoals: Atlantic mackerel, Atlantic horse mackerel, European sprat, European sardine, European anchovy, sand smelt, bogue, salema, garfish, pompano.
  - Tunas: Atlantic bonito, little tunny, bullet tuna, Atlantic bluefin tuna.
  - Cephalopods: European squid, common cuttlefish, common octopus.
  - Sharks and rays of the bottom: small-spotted catshark; thornback ray (released: near threatened).
  - The shallows' sand: lesser weever (released: venomous spines, handled with care).
  - Small fish of the rocks and the estuary, for micro-fishing, every one released: rock goby, shanny, corkwing wrasse, black goby (and the minnow in the streams).
  - Deep holes and wrecks: European hake (a soft mouth: play it gently), John Dory.
  - The big game and Las Brasas: blue shark and shortfin mako (released), albacore, dusky grouper (released), Atlantic chub mackerel.
- **In the game · to be seen, not caught**: the European eel (critically endangered, and banned to leisure fishing in the Gironde): it lives in the estuary's reeds and quay and the bay's rocks, comes out after dark, ignores every bait, and goes in your records when you see it underwater.
- **Planned · not in the game yet**: 9 researched species, previewable in the guide (thin-lipped and golden grey mullet, striped red mullet, shanny, common stingray, allis and twaite shad, sea lamprey, European sturgeon). Protected ones are planned **to watch, not to catch** (sturgeon, allis shad, sea lamprey), and vulnerable ones as **catch and release** (dusky grouper, common stingray, blue shark, thornback ray).
- **Uncertain names** are flagged on the page rather than hidden: the white seabream (WoRMS and the Catalog of Fishes give the Atlantic fish as *Diplodus cadenati*; FishBase and most regional sources still call it *D. sargus*), the bullet tuna (*Auxis rochei*; the frigate tuna also turns up in Iberian catches), the squid and the pompano.

The game's world is one stylised stretch of coast inspired by the whole region, not a map of it, so a southern fish (the urta, the baila, the roncador) can share the water with a northern one; the guide's table of real regions says where each really lives, and how sure that is.

**Known gaps.** Many Cantabrian, Galician and Cádiz statuses rest only on range statements. The plaice's and sprat's presence south of the Bay of Biscay is poorly documented (low confidence), and the flounder's southern limit is disputed and retreating north. No rod-and-line method was found for plaice, sprat or octopus. Some regulations (minimum sizes, closures) change often and are recorded with their dates; check them before relying on them. INPN/TAXREF and several French government pages could not be read by the research and are not cited.

**Identity decisions.** The squid of the coves is the European squid, *Loligo vulgaris* (the usual inshore night-jigging squid; the veined squid *L. forbesii* is deeper and more northern). The bullet tuna is *Auxis rochei* (frigate tuna also turns up in Iberian catches). The garfish is *Belone belone*; the pompano is *Trachinotus ovatus* (several other fish share its Spanish names).

**The fictional originals.** Willow Bay's five invented fish have been replaced in the water by real species in the same roles: glassminnow → sand smelt (*Atherina presbyter*), sunperch → two-banded seabream (*Diplodus vulgaris*), drizzlefin → pollack (*Pollachius pollachius*), duskfin → Atlantic horse mackerel (*Trachurus trachurus*, which now gathers under lights after dark), mossback → European conger (*Conger conger*). The originals no longer live anywhere; catches of them keep their names in the journal and catch log, and the guide shows them as retired, with a link to their replacement, only if you have them in your records.

### Models

Every Atlantic species has an anatomical model built by `src/anatomy.js` from a recipe in `src/anatomy.js` (the first six) or `src/anatomyAtlantic.js` (the rest), following the identification features in the register. Six body plans share the machinery where the animals really are alike:

- **fish**: a body lofted from profiles of depth and width along its length, a mouth cleft (with fleshy lips for the grunts, wrasse and mullet), gill cover, scales and eyes, and fins placed, sized and coloured per species: spiny and soft dorsals, anal, pectorals and pelvics, finlets and keels, and tails forked, truncate, rounded or lunate. The bass's two separate dorsals, the pollack's three, the mackerel's and tunas' finlets, the garfish's long beak, the horse mackerel's row of scutes, the gurnard's blue-edged wings and walking rays, the redbanded seabream's threadlike spines, the pompano's black-tipped lobes;
- **flatfish** (soles, plaice, flounder, turbot): both eyes on top (the turbot's on the left), a fringe of fin running round into the tail, and each species' spot on the pectoral fin;
- **eel** (conger, eel): one long body and one continuous fin, starting just behind the head on the conger and far back on the eel;
- **cuttlefish**, **squid** (rhombic fins, eight arms and two clubbed tentacles) and **octopus** (a mantle bag, eyes with a slot pupil, eight arms that spread and curl).

Each body plan swims its own way, in the vertex shader: a wave down the body for most fish (stiff-bodied, all in the tail, for the tunas), a ripple for flatfish, the whole body for eels, the fin frill for cuttlefish, flapping rear fins for squid, rippling arms for the octopus. In the simulation, shoals keep formation without moving as one, attacked bait balls bulge away from the hunter and re-form, hunting packs (bonito, bluefish, bullet tuna, little tunny, bluefin) drive them to the surface, flatfish lie and glide on the seabed, gurnards and the octopus keep close over it, conger, eel and octopus rest in the rocks by day, and fish holding station face into the current.

Skins are painted from each species' markings at load time; fins are translucent with rays; the sides have a soft sheen. The same model is used in the water, in the guide, on the catch card and in the journal. The five retired fictional fish and the freshwater fish keep their earlier models.

**Asset provenance.** Every model, skin and texture is generated by the code in `src/` (`anatomy.js`, `anatomyAtlantic.js`, `fish.js`, `gearArt.js`, `tackleModels.js`); no external models, textures or photographs are used or shipped. Reference descriptions and photographs on DORIS and FishBase were used only as research for shapes, fins and markings.

**Measured cost** (a 2240 × 1260 view on a laptop; render time per frame, GPU-synchronised, with the browser pane in the background, so read them as relative): about 2,160 animals live in the water and their simulation costs about 0.2 ms per 30 Hz step. In Willow Bay on High, 133 fish in view make 124,000 triangles in 14 instanced draws (a detailed model near the camera, about 1,300–3,700 triangles by species, and a 600-triangle one further off, with no draw at all for a species with none in view), which made no measurable difference to a 16.5 ms render; from inside a sardine shoal underwater, 72,000 fish triangles cost under 0.1 ms on High and 0.75 ms of a 3 ms render on Low. Opening and closing the guide repeatedly, turning every reference model, leaves the studio's GPU memory where it was. The register adds about 93 KB (compressed) to the download and takes about 6 ms to load; the shelter map for the waves is baked in about 0.15 s at start.

### Expansion plan

Done: the six reference species (the first milestone), then the playable roster of 44 with the eel to watch (*The Living Atlantic*), the big game and Las Brasas' own (blue shark, mako, albacore, dusky grouper, chub mackerel), and the angling depth release: the thornback ray (with its own plating fight), the small-spotted catshark (rolling in the line), the lesser weever (venomous, handled with care), the soft-mouthed European hake and the John Dory. What could come next, in order (the batch is on each planned entry):

2. **Common coastal fish still missing**: striped red mullet, thin-lipped and golden grey mullet. (The shanny came in with micro-fishing, with the rock goby, black goby and corkwing wrasse: research/register/batch-f.json.)
3. **Rarer visitors**: the common stingray (to be released: venomous, and vulnerable).

Alongside: seasons (spawning runs, the tuna's summer in Biscay, the meagre's spring in the Gironde, the pompano's summer in the north), a tide to turn the currents, and anatomical models for the freshwater fish.

## Making it your own

- **Add a fish**: research it into `research/register/` and run `node tools/build-register.mjs`, then add an entry to `FISH` in `src/fish.js` with the same id and mark it in `GAME` in `src/register.js`. Its `life` block says where it lives (`homes`, `zones`, `depth`), how it moves, when it is active, how it likes a bait to move, and how cautious it is; `food` says which baits, lures and flies it takes and how keenly; `strike` says how it takes them; `mouth` and `pull` set what size of lure it can manage and how hard it fights. The ecosystem spawns it, and the journal, catch card, 3D model and fish finder pick it up automatically.
- **Add tackle**: add an entry to `TACKLE` in `src/tackle.js` (a bait, lure or fly), give it a model in `src/tackleModels.js`, and add it to the `food` of the fish that should like it. Rods live in `RODS`.
- **More or fewer fish**: `schools`/`members` and `groups`/`group` in each species' `life`, or `fishDensity` in `src/quality.js`.
- **Change the length of a day**: `HOUR_SECONDS` in `src/main.js` (40 real seconds per in-game hour by default).
- **Retune the light**: the `KEYS` table at the top of `src/world/sky.js` sets sky, sun and ambient colours for each part of the day.
- **Reshape the land**: `landSDF` in `src/world/terrain.js`; the lake (`LAKE`), stream (`STREAM`), camp (`CAMP`), path (`PATH`), Longmere (`MERE`), Gannet Isle (`GANNET`) and the rest are constants near the top.
- **Add a boat**: add a hull to `HULLS` in `src/boat.js` (shape, seat, how it moves) and its mooring in `mooring()` in `src/main.js`.
- **Change what's saved**: add the field to `DEFAULT_STATE` and `sanitize` in `src/save.js`. If an old save needs converting, raise `SAVE_VERSION` and add a step to `MIGRATIONS`.
- **Add an adventure**: add an entry to `ADVENTURES` in `src/adventures.js` (a clue, steps with an optional `check` against the game, an ending and a reward). Steps the world settles by itself are checked twice a second; others are recorded where they happen. A notice-board adventure appears on the board by itself.
- **Add a camp decoration**: add it to `DECOR` in `src/campsite.js` and give it a model in `furnitureParts` in `src/campView.js`.
- **Add a discovery**: copy the lantern pattern in `src/main.js` (`lanternReach` and `takeLantern`) and its prop in `buildCove` in `src/world/world.js`.

The browser console has a small helper for tinkering:

```js
slowTide.setHour(19)          // jump to golden hour
slowTide.setRain(1)           // start rain (0 stops it)
slowTide.teleport(140, -16)   // move the boat straight to the cove
slowTide.walkTo(-99, -2)      // stand at camp (on foot)
slowTide.board()              // back into the boat
slowTide.useSkiffAt(20, 30)   // climb into the motor skiff out in the bay
slowTide.useSkiffAt(330, -470, 0, 'bass') // …or another boat: 'row', 'kayak', 'bass'
slowTide.setWeather('heavy')  // clear, partly, overcast, light, heavy or auto
slowTide.setGraphics('low')   // auto, cinematic, ultra, high, medium or low
slowTide.diving.start('free') // go diving from here ('free' or 'scuba'), if there's deep water by you
slowTide.firstPerson()        // into first person (firstPerson(false) to come back out)
slowTide.setWind(0.6)         // a fresh breeze (0 to 1)
slowTide.gear('fly', 'nymph') // hand yourself a rod and tackle
slowTide.reset()              // start a new game (the old save is kept aside)
slowTide.saveInfo()           // how loading went and when the game last saved
slowTide.advance(10)          // run the game forward 10 seconds (works even in a background tab)
slowTide.eco.agents           // every fish in the world, with its species, size, position and state
```

## Ideas for next steps

**On the desktop, for Steam**: `desktop/` holds an Electron wrapper that runs these same files in a window of its own (served from a private `app://` address, full speed in the background, F11 for full screen), with what Steam still needs listed in [desktop/README.md](desktop/README.md). It hasn't been signed up for Steam or run on real machines yet.

**The road to 1.0** (what the game still needs to be worth €9.99, the agreed plan and its order, and open-source projects that could help) is in [ROADMAP.md](ROADMAP.md).

The journal hints at a softer cushion, a potted fern on the bow, and a little cabin by the lake. Other natural next steps: carrying the brass lantern when you walk at night, tides that cover and uncover the estuary flats (and Shell Strand), a second camping kit, cooking and resting at the far camps as at home, and simple upgrades for the motorboats. For the sea, see the [expansion plan](#expansion-plan).
